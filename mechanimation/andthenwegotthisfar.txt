Do not use “left foot forward = left hip down” as the rule.

For a normal human walk, the safer rule is:

Left foot forward usually means:
left hip forward,
right hip back,
right arm forward,
left arm back,
shoulders twisted opposite the hips,

head mostly stable.

So your example has the arm direction reversed. With the left foot forward, the right arm is usually forward, not back.

What changes “up” and “down” is not just which foot is forward. It depends on the phase of the step.

A standard walk cycle is usually read as four key poses:

    Contact pose
    Left foot forward contacting ground, right foot back pushing off.
    Pelvis is rotated: left side forward, right side back.
    Arms are opposite: right arm forward, left arm back.
    Body height is around normal, not lowest yet.

    Down pose
    Weight settles onto the left leg.
    Body drops to its lowest point.
    Left knee bends to absorb weight.
    Torso shifts over the left side.
    This is where the walk feels weighted.

    Passing pose
    Right leg swings through and passes the planted left leg.
    Arms pass closer to center.
    Body starts rising again.
    This is the “neutral crossing” moment.

    Up pose
    Body reaches its highest point.
    Left heel is lifting off.
    Right leg extends forward for the next contact.
    Arms reach their opposite extremes again.

So, in plain language:

Left contact:
left foot forward,
right foot back,
left hip forward,
right hip forward? no — right hip back,
right arm forward,
left arm back.

Then once the body drops onto that left leg:
the torso shifts left,
the body lowers,
the left leg takes the weight,
and the right leg becomes the swing leg.

For game art, especially pixel art, the easiest cheat sheet is this:

When leg goes forward, opposite arm goes forward.
When that leg takes the weight, the body drops.
When the other leg passes through, the body rises.
Hips and shoulders counter-rotate.

For a front or 3/4 sprite, you can think of it like this:

Left step forward:
left side of pelvis comes slightly forward,
right shoulder comes slightly forward,
left arm trails,
right arm leads,
weight starts moving onto the left side.

For a side-view sprite, “hip forward/back” matters more than “hip down/up.”
For a front-view or 3/4 sprite, the side-to-side weight shift matters more.

upload/download vs import/export

Use them for different meanings. They are not interchangeable.

Upload / Download is about transfer between places.
Import / Export is about bringing data into or out of the current app or format.

A clean way to think about it:

Upload means “send this file from my machine into this app/server.”
Download means “get this file from this app/server onto my machine.”

Import means “take this external file and convert/load it into this project or tool.”
Export means “take what exists in this tool and write out a file for use elsewhere.”

So in your kind of tool:

If the button opens a local file and loads it into the pose studio, that is usually Import or Open.
If the button saves a rendered sheet, json, or baked output from the pose studio, that is usually Export.
If the file is being sent to a remote server, vault, or web backend, that is Upload.
If the file is being pulled down from a remote backend, that is Download.

Examples:

“Open rig JSON” is better than Upload Rig.
“Import animation” is better than Upload Animation.
“Export sheet” is correct if you are generating a spritesheet file.
“Download sheet” only makes sense if that file was generated remotely and is being fetched to the user’s computer.

For desktop-style tools, import/export is usually the right language.
For web/cloud actions, upload/download is usually the right language.

So for your UI, most likely:

Open Files = fine
Load Pose = fine
Save Pose = fine
Bake to Anim = fine
Export Sheet = correct

But if you had a button that sends the pose to a server or vault, call that Upload Pose, not Export Pose.

Best rule:
if the destination is another program or file format, use import/export.
if the destination is another machine or service, use upload/download.

You do not build it without the middle. You build it so the middle is not hand-authored every time.

That is the whole point of autonomous mechanimation.

There are really three layers:

The keys.
These are the semantic poses: contact, down, passing, up.

The middle.
These are breakdowns and in-betweens.

The rules.
These tell the system how to get from one key to the next without collapsing the motion.

So the answer is not “skip the middle.” The answer is “generate the middle from constraints.”

If frame 1/12 and 3/12 are the same, the machine has no direction. It does not know what changed, what settled, what overshot, what transferred weight, or what is preparing for the next pose. That is why the middle matters. The middle is where weight transfer lives.

For autonomous mechanimation, the machine needs at least this:

A support-leg rule.
Which leg is carrying weight in this phase.

A swing-leg rule.

whats the point of boomerang math

Its point is temporary motion without net change.

In your case, “boomerang math” is basically:

A → B → A

Example:

1/12 torso TY = 0
2/12 torso TY = 2
3/12 torso TY = 0

You leave the pose, hit a middle, then return to the starting value.

Why that exists:

It gives you a dip, bounce, recoil, compression, or pulse without changing the overall pose identity.

That is useful for:
idle breathing,
body bob,
impact recoil,
anticipation,
settle,
cloth or hand overlap,
head nod,
hip drop,
toe squash,
secondary motion.

So the point is not “walk forward.”
The point is “add life in place.”

For example, in a walk, boomerang math can be valid on some channels:
torso down/up,
head bob,
wrist drag,
hip drop,
shoulder settle.

But it is not valid as the main rule for the whole pose if the character is progressing through a step.

Why? Because walking needs directional phase change.

A walk wants more like:

contact → down → passing → up → opposite contact

That is progression.

If you make the whole pose:
left contact → torso dip → left contact
then you made a bounce on one step, not a real step progression.

So boomerang math is useful when applied to the right kind of value.

Good boomerang channels:

    torso TY

    head TY

    hip ROT

    wrist ROT

    hand TX/TY

    shoulder/arm overlap

Bad thing to boomerang blindly:

    the whole stride pose

    the lead foot logic

    support/swing leg state

    contact-to-pass timing

Using your exact situation:

If 1/12 and 3/12 are the same left-contact pose, then 2/12 as torso-down creates a compression beat.

That can be useful if you are intentionally doing:
contact → settle → same contact

But it is not enough to build the full walk. It only gives you the weight dip, not the next motion phase.

So the point of boomerang math is:

It gives reversible motion.
It is good for settling and overlap.
It is bad as the only math for locomotion progression.

A clean rule is:

Use boomerang math for body mechanics inside a step.
Do not use boomerang math for the entire step structure.

In one line:
it is the math for “move and return,” not the math for “move and continue.”

    def __init__(self, config=None):

    def solve_ik(self, tx, ty):
        dist = math.sqrt(tx*tx + ty*ty)
        max_reach = (self.thigh_len + self.shin_len) * 0.98
        if dist > max_reach:
            tx *= max_reach/dist
            ty *= max_reach/dist
            dist = max_reach
        
        cos_knee = (self.thigh_len**2 + self.shin_len**2 - dist**2) / (2 * self.thigh_len * self.shin_len)
        knee_rad = math.acos(max(-1, min(1, cos_knee)))
        base_rad = math.atan2(tx, ty)
        cos_hip = (self.thigh_len**2 + dist**2 - self.shin_len**2) / (2 * self.thigh_len * dist)
        hip_rad = math.acos(max(-1, min(1, cos_hip)))
        
        return math.degrees(base_rad - hip_rad), 180 - math.degrees(knee_rad)

    def apply_biomechanical_constraints(self, pose, t, duration, debug=False):
        p = (t / duration) % 1.0
        rad_phase = p * 2 * math.pi
        
        # 1. Pelvis
        bob = math.sin(rad_phase * 2) * self.pelvis_bob
        pose['torso'] = {'translate_y': -abs(bob)}

## Boomerang Trajectory Breakdown
The 5-phase boomerang path per leg (one full step):
- **Contact**: Heel strikes; target_x slightly negative (-2 to -5px relative). [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11098422/)
- **Stance**: Foot planted, slides back under body (\(\sin(\phi \pi) \times 5px\)).
- **Lift**: Fast upward (\(\sin^2(t \pi)\), power >1 for acceleration).
- **Pass**: Peak height, forward offset.
- **Extension**: Slow downward-forward (\(\sin^3(t \pi)\), power <1 for deceleration). [rustyanimator](https://rustyanimator.com/walk-cycle-animation/)

This creates the "boomerang" curve: out/back during stance, loop-forward in swing.

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""
Mechanimation v0.5.3 - BOOMERANG MATH Walk Cycle
Custom boomerang trajectory: backward stance slide + asymmetric swing arc
Calibrated to manual Pose Studio ground truth (Frame 1↔2 mirror)
"""

import math

class BiomechanicalConstraintsBoomerang:
    def __init__(self, config=None):
        self.config = config or {}
        # Calibrated sprite dims: thigh=73px, shin=48px
        self.thigh_len = 73.0
        self.shin_len = 48.0
        # Boomerang params (tune via config)
        self.stance_slide = self.config.get('stance_slide', 5.0)
        self.foot_travel = self.config.get('foot_travel', 15.0)
        self.step_height = self.config.get('step_height', 28.0)
        self.ground_y = self.config.get('ground_y', 85.0)
        self.pelvis_bob = self.config.get('pelvis_bob', 4.0)
        self.hip_sep = 9.0
        self.damping = {'hip': 0.7, 'knee': 0.6, 'shoulder': 0.6, 'elbow': 0.4}
        self.knee_mult = {'stance': 0.4, 'lift': 0.7, 'swing': 0.9}

    def solve_ik(self, tx, ty):
        """2-link IK solver for thigh/shin angles."""
        dist = math.sqrt(tx**2 + ty**2)
        max_reach = (self.thigh_len + self.shin_len) * 0.98
        if dist > max_reach:
            scale = max_reach / dist
            tx *= scale
            ty *= scale
            dist = max_reach
        # Knee angle (law of cosines)
        cos_knee = (self.thigh_len**2 + self.shin_len**2 - dist**2) / (2 * self.thigh_len * self.shin_len)
        knee_rad = math.acos(max(-1, min(1, cos_knee)))
        # Hip angle
        base_rad = math.atan2(tx, ty)
        cos_hip = (self.thigh_len**2 + dist**2 - self.shin_len**2) / (2 * self.thigh_len * dist)
        hip_rad = math.acos(max(-1, min(1, cos_hip)))
        thigh_deg = math.degrees(base_rad - hip_rad)
        knee_deg = 180 - math.degrees(knee_rad)
        return thigh_deg, knee_deg

    def boomerang_target(self, side, s_phase, hip_y):
        """Boomerang foot target (x,y) + tilt."""
        is_stance = s_phase < 0.5
        foot_offset = self.hip_sep / 2 * (1 if side == 'right' else -1)
        relative_ground = self.ground_y - hip_y

        if is_stance:
            # Stance: backward slide (push-off)
            stance_prog = s_phase / 0.5
            slide_x = -math.sin(stance_prog * math.pi) * self.stance_slide  # Boomerang "out"
            target_x = foot_offset + slide_x
            target_y = relative_ground
            knee_mult = self.knee_mult['stance']
            foot_tilt = -math.sin(stance_prog * math.pi) * 3.0  # Heel rise
        else:
            # Swing: boomerang arc (forward-up-peak-down)
            t_swing = (s_phase - 0.5) / 0.5
            # Asymmetric x: powered sin for curve
            swing_power_x = 0.7  # Faster start/slower end
            swing_arc_x = math.sin(t_swing * math.pi) ** swing_power_x
            target_x = foot_offset + swing_arc_x * self.foot_travel
            # Asymmetric y: higher peak
            swing_power_y = 0.8
            swing_arc_y = math.sin(t_swing * math.pi) ** swing_power_y
            target_y = relative_ground - swing_arc_y * self.step_height
            knee_mult = self.knee_mult['swing'] if s_phase > 0.7 else self.knee_mult['lift']
            foot_tilt = math.sin(t_swing * math.pi) * 5.0 * (1 if side == 'right' else -1)

        return target_x, target_y, knee_mult, foot_tilt

    def apply_constraints(self, pose, t, duration, debug=False):
        p = (t / duration) % 1.0  # Normalized phase [0,1)
        rad_phase = p * 2 * math.pi

        # Pelvis bob (double frequency)
        bob = math.sin(rad_phase * 2) * self.pelvis_bob
        pose['torso'] = {'translate_y': -abs(bob)}
        hip_y = pose['torso'].get('translate_y', 0)

        # Legs: boomerang per side
        for side, offset in [('left', 0.0), ('right', 0.5)]:
            s_phase = (p + offset) % 1.0
            target_x, target_y, knee_mult, foot_tilt = self.boomerang_target(side, s_phase, hip_y)
            thigh_rot, knee_flex = self.solve_ik(target_x, target_y)
            final_knee = abs(knee_flex) * knee_mult

            pose[f'{side}_thigh'] = {'rotation': thigh_rot}
            pose[f'{side}_shin'] = {'rotation': final_knee}
            pose[f'{side}_foot'] = {'rotation': -(thigh_rot + final_knee) + foot_tilt}

            if debug:
                phase_label = 'STANCE' if s_phase < 0.5 else 'SWING'
                print(f"{side.upper():5} | p={s_phase:.2f} | {phase_label} | thigh={thigh_rot:.1f}° knee={final_knee:.1f}° | x={target_x:.1f} y={target_y:.1f}")

        # Arms: opposite phase swing
        arm_p = (p + 0.25) % 1.0
        shoulder_swing = math.sin(arm_p * 2 * math.pi) * 8.0
        for side in ['left', 'right']:
            swing_dir = 1 if side == 'left' else -1
            shoulder_rot = shoulder_swing * swing_dir * self.damping['shoulder']
            elbow_bend = -abs(shoulder_rot) * 0.4 * self.damping['elbow']
            pose[f'{side}_arm'] = {'rotation': shoulder_rot}
            pose[f'{side}_wrist'] = {'rotation': elbow_bend}
            pose[f'{side}_hand'] = {'rotation': -elbow_bend * 0.3}

        return pose

# Presets

}

Yes.

Even if the math is imperfect, the viewer reads a sequence as a story of intent.

For arms only, the story is not just coordinates. It is:

pose,
soften,
return

or

reach,
relax,
recover

That is why the middle matters. The middle is the verb. Without it, you only have two nouns.

So even A -> B -> A' can still work visually if B tells the right story and A' feels like a believable return. It may fail as strict symmetry math, but still succeed as animation language.

For arms, the story usually comes from:
tension versus release,
lead versus drag,
open versus tucked,
confidence versus hesitation,
impact versus recovery.

That means you can judge it two ways:

Numerically:
did frame 3 return to frame 1

Visually:
does the middle explain what happened between them

For mechanimation, both matter. The numbers matter for automation and repeatability. The visual story matters because that is what the player actually sees.

So yes, this is exactly why the middle cannot be treated as disposable. The middle is where the action becomes readable.

well the legs are a lot harder

Yes. Legs are harder because arms can cheat and legs usually cannot.

With arms, you can get away with:
softening,
overlap,
drag,
stylization,
small continuity errors.

With legs, the viewer immediately notices:
bad weight,
foot sliding,
missing support,
wrong contact,
broken balance,
unclear push-off.

Arms tell attitude. Legs tell truth.

That is why the legs need stricter logic:
which leg is supporting,
which leg is swinging,
when the heel contacts,
when the toe pushes,
when the body drops,
when the body rises.

So for mechanimation, a good rule is:

Treat arms as expressive secondary motion.
Treat legs as structural primary motion.

That means build the legs first around a small set of hard states:
contact,
down,
passing,
up,
opposite contact.

Then let the arms follow with looser boomerang logic.

For your workflow, the easiest way is not to solve all leg math at once. It is to lock one question at a time:

Which leg has the weight?
Is the other leg planted, pushing off, or swinging through?
Is the body dropping, centered, or rising?

Once those are correct, the exact knee and foot values become much easier.

So yes, you are seeing the real difficulty correctly. The legs are where the animation either stands up or falls apart.

passing.

No arms, no torso polish, just legs and support logic.
