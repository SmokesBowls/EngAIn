ACT 4: THE CLAY HEART — ENTER KULLA

The heist was going wrong.

They had the shuttle. Oreck had won his brutal fight, Kyh had sliced through the encryption, and Geralt had woven a tapestry of tiny, guaranteed moments to get them aboard. But as they powered up the engines, the lunar ground beneath the hangar rejected them.

It wasn’t an explosion. It was a failure. Support struts buckled not with violence, but with sudden, profound fatigue. The hangar door’s gears ground to a halt, seized by grit that hadn’t been there a second before. The very rock seemed to be… objecting.

From a service shaft, something pulled itself into the hangar bay.

It was a man of clay and brick. A grotesque, asymmetical patchwork of fired earth, mortar, and rusted rebar. One leg was solid basalt, the other a crumbling mix of mud and wire. Cracks webworked across its torso, shedding fine dust. Its face was a crude impression in clay, thumb-sockets for eyes that held a spark of weary, ancient intelligence.

It moved with a grinding, painful slowness, each step a deliberate act of architecture. It stopped between them and the open sky.

“That ship,” its voice emerged like stones grinding in a quarry, “is poorly made. Its thrust harmonics will fracture the local tectonics for a kilometer down. You can’t leave.”

Oreck shifted into a combat stance, snarling. “Stand aside, spirit.”

The thing—Kulla—tilted its mismatched head. “I am not a spirit. I am the foreman. And you,” its thumb-socket eyes fixed on Geralt, specifically on the Dragon Mail, “are adding unstable weight to a stressed foundation. Your destiny is a load-bearing wall in a design I did not approve.”

Geralt felt no cosmic power from it. No vast consciousness. Only a deep, specific, and intensely physical grievance. This wasn’t a god. It was a forgotten tool, awake and furious at the shoddy workmanship of the new age.

“We have to go,” Geralt said, holding out a hand to stay Oreck. “A greater catastrophe is unfolding. We must reach the asteroid belt.”

“I know what unfolds there,” Kulla rumbled, taking a step that sheared clay from its knee. “They are building on a fault line of madness. They are using bad mortar.” It sounded less like a prophecy and more like a craftsman’s scathing report. “You will not stop them by adding another vibrating engine to the chaos. You will stop them by removing the keystone.”

It pointed a lumpy, brick-ended finger not at the shuttle, but at a seemingly random support column in the hangar. “That one. It is the only honest work in this whole place. It holds the memory of correct angles. I will let you pass if you promise to take me. My body is failing. This moon is weak stone. The belt… has better materials. I can repair myself there. And I can show you what really holds the Shattered Mind together.”

The offer hung in the dusty air. A mud-man, asking for a ride to a cosmic crime scene, not to fight a queen, but to critique her masonry.

Geralt looked at this pathetic, powerful, literal embodiment of forgotten foundation. He saw in Kulla a mirror: another being created for a purpose, buried by progress, now clawing its way out to do its job one last time. His training allowed him to see the thread. Kulla was inelastic. A truth of stone and pressure. Not an ally to be won, but a fact to be incorporated.

“Get in,” Geralt said.

As Kulla shuffled toward the shuttle, moving with the devastating, patient speed of continental drift, Oreck shot Geralt a look.

“A mud man,” the reptilian muttered.
“A foundation,” Geralt replied, the numbness in his voice allowing for no argument. “And we are about to build on very shaky ground.”

The shuttle lifted off, its stolen engines flaring. Below, the compromised hangar, missing its one "honest" column, quietly subsided into the regolith. Kulla watched it collapse through the viewport, his expression unreadable in clay.

“See?” he grumbled. “Told you.”

The journey to the asteroid belt had begun. They had their shuttle, their trail, and their first, bewildering new ally. The next step was the chaos.

And waiting in that chaos, Geralt knew, would be the ghosts of his past, ready to speak. The Five Mikas would have something to say.
