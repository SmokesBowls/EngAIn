  Years Pass  

The great forests became graveyards of frozen wood. The mighty rivers turned to ribbons of ice that never thawed. The primitive human settlements that had once dotted the landscape vanished beneath snow drifts that grew deeper with each passing season.

But the Igigi endured.

Scattered across the dying continents, they used their consciousness-crafting abilities to sustain themselves—converting mental energy into thermal regulation, transforming thought-patterns into nutrition synthesis, burning the very essence of their identity to maintain biological functions in a world that had become hostile to all complex life.

Zephyr learned to reshape his mathematical consciousness into survival algorithms, calculating optimal energy expenditure for each movement, each breath, each heartbeat. His elegant equations, once used to birth star systems, now focused on the mundane arithmetic of staying alive another day.

The loneliness was worse than the cold.

In the quantum foam, he could sometimes sense the others—distant points of consciousness struggling with the same endless calculations of survival. But the distances were too great, the energy cost of communication too high. Each refugee faced the grinding darkness alone, their numbers slowly diminishing as some made the final calculation that cessation was preferable to continuation.

Zephyr persisted not through hope but through mathematical stubbornness. The numbers said survival was possible, barely, if every action was optimized, if every thought was disciplined, if every moment was calculated with precision. So he calculated. Day after day, year after year, his consciousness becoming a machine for extracting survival from impossibility.

It was during his fourteenth year of isolation that the blizzard came.

Not the ordinary storms that had become routine, but something worse—a weather system so violent that even his mathematical mind couldn't process all the variables. Wind velocities that defied his calculations. Temperature drops that fell below his survival thresholds. A maelstrom of ice and fury that would make survival impossible for even a consciousness-crafter of his abilities.

He had perhaps minutes before his thermal-regulation systems would be overwhelmed. Minutes to find shelter, to reach safety, to avoid becoming another point of light that simply stopped shining in the quantum foam.

The equations were clear: zero-point-zero-zero percent survival probability.

Unless...

Through the howling wind and driving snow, he sensed something. Not another Igigi—their bio-signatures were familiar to his consciousness. This was something else. Something vast and ancient and touched with power that didn't operate on the same mathematical principles as Anunnaki technology.

A Giant.

The primitive species that had inhabited this world before the Anunnaki brought winter. Most had died in the early years of the freeze, but some had adapted, their consciousness expanding to encompass magics that didn't follow logical rules. Tidecaller Giants, they were called, beings who commanded water and storm through intuition rather than calculation.

In any other circumstances, Zephyr would have avoided contact. Giants and Igigi had been enemies in the old wars, their approaches to consciousness-crafting fundamentally incompatible. Mathematics versus mysticism. Logic versus intuition. Structure versus chaos.

But the blizzard cared nothing for ancient enmities.

As his thermal systems began to fail and the equations of his doom crystallized with mathematical certainty, Zephyr made a choice that would have seemed impossible fourteen years ago.

He called out through the storm, not in the language of mathematics, but in the crude emotional frequencies that Giants understood.

 Help. 

The response came not as calculation but as presence—vast, warm, patient. A consciousness that approached the storm not as a problem to be solved but as a force to be understood, worked with, shaped through harmony rather than opposition.

Through the driving snow, a massive form materialized. Eight feet tall, covered in fur and ice, eyes that held depths Zephyr's mathematical mind couldn't fathom. A Tidecaller Giant, one of the few who had somehow survived the endless winter by learning to dance with forces that refused to be calculated.

Their eyes met across species-lines, across ancient hatreds, across the howling wind that was killing them both.

And in that moment, the first impossible friendship began.
