

 📖 CHAPTER 30: "THE CRASH SITE"


The island had stood unchanged for three millennia, a sanctuary carved from probability itself where the boundaries between what was and what could be grew thin as morning mist. Zephyr—who had been called Sage, Teacher, the Nameless One, and a dozen other titles across civilizations that had risen and fallen like tides—stood at its eastern shore, his ancient eyes fixed on the horizon where smoke stained the sky.

"You felt it too," Torn rumbled beside him, the giant's voice carrying harmonics of stone grinding against stone. At twelve feet tall, Torn was small for his kind, but what he lacked in stature he made up for in presence. Scars from countless battles marked his granite-grey skin, each one a story, each story a lesson learned the hard way.

"A falling star," Zephyr replied, though both knew it was no star. The cosmic frequencies that had torn through reality hours earlier spoke of technology, desperation, and violence. "Or a falling prison."

Torn grunted acknowledgment. "Anunnaki vessel. I recognize the dimensional signature—they leave wounds when they travel." He flexed massive hands. "The question is: do we care?"

It was a fair question. The island existed outside normal causality, a bubble where time moved according to different rules. They could ignore this, let whatever catastrophe had befallen the crashed vessel play out without their involvement. The universe was full of tragedies, and even an immortal sage couldn't prevent them all.

But Zephyr had felt something else in those cosmic frequencies—something familiar. A resonance he'd encountered before, decades ago in a war that had reshaped continents. Geralt's signature, faint but unmistakable. And where Geralt went, chaos followed.

"We care," Zephyr said simply, turning from the shore. "Pack light. We move fast."

Torn didn't argue. For all his gruff exterior, the giant trusted Zephyr's instincts. Three thousand years of partnership had taught him that when the Sage said to move, you moved.

They left the island as the sun reached its zenith, reality rippling around them like heat shimmer as they stepped from sanctuary into the mundane world. The transition always felt like waking from a dream—suddenly the weight of normal physics reasserted itself, time resumed its linear tyranny, and the vast possibilities of the island collapsed into the singular now of mortal existence.

"South-southwest," Zephyr said, orienting by senses that had nothing to do with eyes. "Through the Cerulean Waters, across the Southern Wastes. Two days if we're fortunate. Three if the desert decides to be uncooperative."

"The desert is always uncooperative," Torn muttered, but fell into step beside the Sage.

The first day passed in relative silence, both lost in their own thoughts as they crossed terrain that shifted from coastal scrubland to the rocky shores of the Cerulean Sea. Zephyr moved with a grace that belied his apparent age—he appeared as an old man, weathered and white-haired, but his steps never faltered, his breathing never labored. Torn, despite his size, navigated the landscape with surprising agility, his giant heritage granting him an intuitive understanding of stone and earth.

By nightfall, they'd reached the water's edge.

"We rest here," Zephyr announced, studying the dark waters. "The Brajor will notice our crossing. Better to meet them refreshed than exhausted."

Torn grunted agreement and set about making camp—a generous term for clearing a flat space and sitting down. Giants didn't need much comfort. Zephyr, for his part, spent the evening in meditation, his consciousness expanding outward to taste the probability currents that flowed around the crashed vessel.

What he found troubled him.

Multiple factions converging. Federation oversight. Anunnaki recovery teams. Scavengers drawn by opportunity. And underneath it all, five signatures that flickered like dying embers—familiar, important, and rapidly fading.

"Survivors," he murmured. "But not for long."

"Then we'd better hurry," Torn replied from across the camp, proving once again that giant hearing was sharper than most assumed.

They set out at dawn, wading into the Cerulean Waters as the sun painted the eastern sky in shades of amber and rose. The water here was shallow for nearly a mile, barely reaching Torn's knees and Zephyr's waist, warm as bathwater and clear as glass. Fish darted away from their passage, silver flashes that caught the morning light.

They were perhaps halfway across when the water ahead began to churn.

Torn's hand moved to the massive war-club strapped to his back, but Zephyr raised a calming hand. "Peace, old friend. These are not enemies."

A figure broke the surface twenty yards ahead—humanoid but distinctly other, with pale skin that seemed to shimmer between flesh and water itself. More figures emerged, forming a loose semi-circle around them. The Brajor.

The central figure moved closer, and Zephyr recognized the spiritual weight that accompanied true leadership. Female, though gender was somewhat fluid among the Brajor, with eyes that held the depth of abyssal trenches.

"Lyra Tidecaller," Zephyr greeted her with a slight bow of respect. "Forgive the intrusion into your waters."

The Brajor leader studied them both, her gaze lingering on Torn with curiosity before returning to Zephyr. Recognition flickered across her features—not personal recognition, but the acknowledgment of power recognizing power.

"Sage Zephyr," she replied, her voice carrying harmonics that resonated through the water itself. "The tides whispered of your coming. A disturbance in the heavens, a wound in the sky. We felt it."

"As did we." Zephyr gestured toward the smoke-stained horizon. "Something fell. Something... significant."

Lyra's expression darkened. "The scaled ones have been agitated. Their scouts pass through our waters, heading toward the impact site. The Federation's ships hover overhead like vultures waiting for death." She paused, studying him. "You go to help."

It wasn't a question.

"If help is possible," Zephyr acknowledged.

For a long moment, Lyra said nothing, her ancient eyes searching his face for something. Finally, she nodded. "The Brajor do not forget debts. You

 aided us during the Third Cleansing, when the deep-spawn rose from the abyss. We offer safe passage and..." she hesitated, "...perhaps more, should you need it."

"Your friendship is gift enough," Zephyr replied. "Though if your scouts hear of anything unusual at the crash site—survivors in need, conflicts arising—word sent to the Southern Wastes would be appreciated."

"It will be done." Lyra gestured, and the semi-circle of Brajor parted, creating a clear path. "May the currents guide you, Sage. And may whatever you find be worth the finding."

They crossed the remaining water in peace, the Brajor watching until they reached the far shore. Zephyr glanced back once to see Lyra still standing in the shallows, her form already beginning to merge back into the water itself.

"Good people," Torn observed as they climbed onto dry land.

"The best often are," Zephyr agreed. "Remember them, old friend. The coming days will need allies, and the Brajor remember their debts as clearly as their grievances."

Beyond the Cerulean Waters lay the Southern Wastes—a name that did the region a grave disservice. Yes, it was desert, but it was far from wasted. Life thrived here for those who knew where to look: hardy scrub brush that could survive months without rain, insects that had evolved a thousand ingenious ways to conserve water, and predators perfectly adapted to hunt in the merciless heat.

Including the Dregons.

They encountered the first pack three hours into the Wastes, as the sun reached its apex and turned the sand into a griddle that would have been unbearable for anyone less resilient. Six of them, lean and mean, with vestigial wing-structures that spoke of an evolutionary path toward flight that had never quite succeeded.

"Territorial," Torn observed unnecessarily as the Dregons began their threat display—spreading those useless wings, hissing to reveal rows of needle-sharp teeth, stamping clawed feet in the sand.

"And stupid," Zephyr added as the lead Dregon lunged.

What followed was less a fight than an educational demonstration in why attacking ancient cosmic beings and their giant companions was inadvisable. Zephyr didn't even use magic—a simple redirection of momentum sent the lead Dregon tumbling past him into the sand. Torn's contribution was more direct: one massive backhand that sent a Dregon flying twenty feet.

The pack retreated, hissing and snarling, pride wounded but survival instinct intact.

"Buttholes," Torn summarized succinctly.

"Always have been," Zephyr agreed, brushing sand from his robes. "Though I suppose we're trespassing in their hunting grounds. From their perspective, we're the buttholes."

"Philosophy later. Walking now."

They pushed deeper into the Wastes, the crash site drawing closer with each mile. By late afternoon, they could see it: a scar across the landscape, a canyon of torn earth and burning metal that stretched for miles. The ship—and "ship" seemed inadequate for something the size of a small city—had hit at an angle, carving through desert and stone before finally embedding itself into the foothills of the northern mountains.

And from those mountains, Torn saw his distant kin.

"Mountain Giants," he breathed, stopping in his tracks. Even at this distance, they were visible—three of them, each easily twice Torn's height, moving down from their highland homes to investigate the intrusion into their territory.

Zephyr studied his companion's face, reading the conflict there. "You should go to them."

"But the crash site—"

"—will still be there. Your people won't appreciate a lowland giant arriving with an unknown mage and ignoring diplomatic courtesy. Go. Explain we mean no harm. Smooth the way." Zephyr's smile was gentle. "I can handle a crash site, old friend."

Torn hesitated, torn between duty to his companion and responsibility to his distant kin. Finally, he nodded. "Don't do anything stupid without me."

"When have I ever?"

"Do you want the list chronologically or by severity?"

Despite everything, Zephyr laughed. "Go. I'll see you on the other side of this mess."

He watched Torn lumber off toward the mountain giants, then turned his full attention to the crash site sprawling before him.

Time to see what tragedy the universe had delivered today.

The crash site wasn't just chaos—it was a layered catastrophe that spoke to the violence of the ship's final moments. The vessel had carved a canyon through the desert, each section telling a different story of destruction. Zephyr approached methodically, his cosmic senses cataloging the devastation while his ancient mind calculated trajectories, impact velocities, and survival probabilities.

The math was grim.

The forward sections had taken the initial impact—complete structural failure, crushed beyond recognition. Moving aft, the damage lessened incrementally, though "lessened" was relative when dealing with a city-sized vessel torn apart by gravity and inertia. Some sections burned with chemical fires that would take days to extinguish. Others lay dark and silent, power completely severed. A few, miraculously, still showed signs of functioning systems—flickering emergency lights, the whine of failing air recyclers, and the omnipresent wail of alarms screaming into the desert air.

And underneath it all, the sounds that drew him forward: voices. Screaming. Crying. The wet cough of someone with punctured lungs. The delirious babbling of shock. Life, clinging desperately to existence amid the wreckage of technology that had failed its passengers.

Zephyr moved toward the sounds.

The first survivor he found was pinned beneath a collapsed bulkhead, conscious but fading. Anunnaki, judging by the bone structure and the faint shimmer of genetic modifications that had once granted enhanced reflexes. Those reflexes hadn't saved him from several tons of falling metal.

"Help," the being rasped, eyes focusing on Zephyr with the desperate clarity of someone who knows death is imminent. "Please..."

Zephyr knelt beside him, hands already glowing with soft golden light. "I'm going to lift this. When I do, crawl out quickly."

"Can't... legs..."

"I'll handle it." Zephyr pressed his palms against the bulkhead. Magic flowed—not the showy, explosive kind that would attract attention, but the subtle manipulation of mass and friction that let physics bend just enough. The bulkhead rose three inches. Then six. The survivor gasped as pressure released, blood flow returning to crushed tissue.

"Now," Zephyr commanded.

The being tried. Failed. Consciousness wavered.

Zephyr made a decision. His other hand moved to the survivor's chest, and healing magic poured through—basic life support, stabilizing the worst internal damage, knitting blood vessels that had torn. Not a complete healing, but enough.

The survivor's eyes cleared. He dragged himself free.

"Thank you," he breathed. "I thought—"

"Find shelter away from the fires. There are others who need help." Zephyr was already moving deeper into the wreckage, following the next cry for help.

The next hour passed in a blur of crisis management. A survivor trapped in a crushed compartment—Zephyr carefully cut through twisted metal with precisely focused heat, pulling the being free before oxygen deprivation caused brain damage. Another survivor with a shard of hull plating through his abdomen—Zephyr stabilized the wound, left the shard in place (removing it would cause immediate death from blood loss), and directed the being toward other survivors who could help.

Three more. Five more. Seven. Each rescue a calculation of time versus benefit, triage decisions made in microseconds. He was careful with his magic—small applications, nothing that would light up like a beacon to whatever surveillance or security forces might still be monitoring the wreckage.

He was moving through what appeared to have been crew quarters when he heard it.

Snoring.

Zephyr stopped mid-step, his ancient mind momentarily convinced his hearing had finally started to fail after three millennia. But no—there it was again. A deep, resonant snore, followed by a lighter, whistling counterpoint. Coming from a compartment that, by some miracle of physics and engineering, had survived the crash almost entirely intact.

He approached cautiously. The door hung open, twisted on its hinges but passable. Inside, two bunks. On those bunks, two men.

Sleeping.

Peacefully.

While the world burned around them.

Zephyr stood in the doorway, processing this impossibility. The ship had crashed. Violently. The sound alone should have woken them. The impact should have thrown them from their bunks. The alarms—still wailing throughout the wreckage—should have penetrated even the deepest sleep.

And yet.

The first man—stocky, with the weathered look of someone who'd done hard labor his entire life—lay on his back, mouth open, producing sounds that would have made a lumber mill envious. The second—leaner, younger perhaps, with scars suggesting a violent past—sprawled across his bunk in a position that looked deeply uncomfortable but apparently wasn't, given the serene expression on his face.

Zephyr cleared his throat. Nothing.

He spoke, voice raised. "Gentlemen."

The stocky one snorted, rolled over, and continued sleeping.

Zephyr tried again, louder. "The ship has crashed."

The lean one's snoring stuttered, resumed.

Exasperated in a way he hadn't felt in centuries, Zephyr reached out with a tiny thread of magic and gave each man a sharp mental ping—the cosmic equivalent of dumping ice water on someone's head.

Both men jolted awake simultaneously, flailing, reaching for weapons that weren't there, eyes wild with the sudden transition from deep sleep to waking panic.

"What—who—don't take us back!" the stocky one shouted, scrambling off his bunk and taking a defensive stance that spoke of fighting experience.

The lean one was faster, already on his feet, hands raised but ready. "We're not going back to the pens!"

Then they both stopped, actually looking at Zephyr for the first time. An old man, weathered and white-haired, wearing simple robes, standing calmly in their doorway with an expression that somehow managed to convey both patience and profound amusement.

Not guards. Not slavers. Not Anunnaki security.

"Who are you?" the stocky one asked, stance relaxing slightly but not completely.

"Someone who finds it remarkable that you slept through a spaceship crash," Zephyr replied dryly.

The two men looked at each other. Then at the twisted doorframe. Then through it, to where smoke and sparks painted the corridor in apocalyptic shades.

"Did we..." the lean one started.

"You did," Zephyr confirmed. "The ship crashed approximately two hours ago. You've been sleeping through the entire disaster."

Another shared look. Then, impossibly, they both started laughing—the slightly hysterical laughter of people whose brains couldn't quite process the absurdity of their situation.

"Kael," the stocky one gasped, pointing at his companion. "How did you sleep through that?"

"How did YOU?" Kael shot back. "I'm a light sleeper! You're the one who could sleep through—" he gestured at the wreckage, "—apparently through exactly this!"

Their laughter trailed off as the reality of the situation sank in. They'd crashed. They'd survived. And they'd been so exhausted from whatever had come before that they'd slept through humanity's worst nightmare.

"We're free," the stocky one—Roric, Zephyr surmised from context—breathed, the weight of the word heavy with meaning. "The ship crashed. The slavers..."

"Are likely dead, scattered, or dealing with their own problems," Zephyr supplied. "You're slaves? Were slaves?"

"Were," Kael confirmed, his voice gaining strength. "If they're dead. If the ship's destroyed. If—" He stopped, a thought occurring to him. He moved to the doorway, looked past Zephyr down the corridor, then beyond to where a massive rent in the hull showed open sky and desert landscape. His breath caught.

"Roric. Look."

Roric joined him at the doorway. Together they stared at the view—not at the destruction, but at what lay beyond it. Mountains rising in the distance, their peaks catching the afternoon light. Between the wreckage and those mountains, a valley stretched out, lush with vegetation that spoke of water sources, shelter, possibility.

No walls. No guards. No chains—literal or otherwise.

Just... space. Freedom. Opportunity.

"We could build there," Kael whispered, and Zephyr heard dreams crystallizing in real-time. "Look at that valley. Water, probably. Good soil. Defensible position in the mountain foothills. We could—"

"A home," Roric finished, his voice thick with emotion. "An actual home. Not a holding pen. Not a work camp. Ours."

They stood there for a long moment, two former slaves seeing possibility where others saw only wilderness. Zephyr watched them, his cosmic senses reading the probability currents that flowed around these two. He saw futures branching—saw a small settlement growing in that valley, saw structures rising from salvaged ship materials, saw these two men becoming founders of something significant.

"You should go," he said quietly. "While the chaos provides cover. Take what supplies you can carry from the wreckage. Head for that valley. Start building."

They turned to him, questions in their eyes, but Zephyr was already moving back down the corridor. He had his own mission, and it didn't involve guiding former slaves toward their destiny. They'd find it themselves, or they wouldn't. Either way, the universe would continue turning.

But as he walked away, he heard Roric's voice, filled with a determination that would reshape this region over the coming years:

"Come on, Kael. Let's go build a home."

Zephyr allowed himself a small smile. At least something good would come from this disaster.

He moved deeper into the wreckage, his senses now focused on a different kind of search. The survivors he'd helped were alive and moving—they'd find their own way out. But there were others. Signatures that didn't pulse with life, but flickered with something else. Something familiar.

The first body he found was in what had been a command section, crushed beneath a fallen support beam. Male, heavily muscled, with armor that bore markings Zephyr recognized. Not Anunnaki standard issue. Something else. Custom work, maintained with the care of someone who understood that good armor was the difference between death and survival.

Zephyr knelt beside the body, reaching out to move debris aside, and froze.

The armor. He knew this armor. Had seen it before, decades ago, on warriors who'd fought in battles that had reshaped continents. And the symbol etched into the breastplate—crude, but distinctive. A stylized flame surrounding a broken chain.

Gerald's symbol. The mark of his inner circle.

"No," Zephyr murmured, though he didn't know if he was denying the identification or lamenting it. His hands moved faster now, clearing debris, examining the body more closely. The face was damaged beyond easy recognition, but the bone structure, the scars, the particular way this warrior's hands had been calloused from specific weapon grips...

"You were one of his," Zephyr said softly to the corpse. "One of the five closest. I remember you from the Battle of Ashren Gate. You held the left flank when everyone else broke. You..."

He trailed off, mind racing. If this one was here, what did that mean? Where was Gerald? Had he been on this ship? Was he—

No. Gerald's signature wasn't among the dead. Zephyr would have sensed it immediately. But his followers... his most loyal...

Zephyr stood, looking around with new purpose. If one of the five was here, the others might be as well. And if they'd been on this ship, that meant Gerald had been involved in whatever had brought it down. Another piece of a puzzle Zephyr didn't yet fully understand.

He found a relatively sheltered alcove, carved out by the impact in a way that created a small cave of twisted metal and shattered bulkhead. Safe enough. Hidden enough. He carried the first body there carefully, laying it with respect due to warriors who'd earned their place in history through blood and loyalty.

Then he continued his search.

The second body was easier to find—the crash had thrown it clear of the main wreckage, and it lay in the open sand like an offering to the desert sky. Female this time, smaller but no less deadly in life, judging by the weapons still strapped to her body. The same armor style. The same symbol. Another of Gerald's five.

Zephyr retrieved her, brought her to his makeshift sanctuary, laid her beside the first.

The third he found in what must have been a cargo bay, crushed between containers that had broken free during the crash. It took careful manipulation of mass to free the body without causing further structural collapse. Another of the five. The cosmic threads that connected these warriors to Gerald pulsed weakly still, not with life, but with that resonance that persisted between those who'd fought together, bled together, died together.

Three out of five. Zephyr's jaw tightened. He was finding a pattern in where the bodies had fallen—they'd been in specific sections of the ship, clustered in ways that suggested they'd been together when the crash happened. Which meant the remaining two were likely nearby.

He was searching a collapsed corridor, moving debris with careful applications of force, when he heard voices.

Human voices. And not the grateful, desperate voices of survivors.

"Check the aft sections. Command said there might be salvage worth taking before the official teams arrive."

"Screw official teams. We were here first. Finder's keepers."

Scavengers. Of course. A crash site this size would draw them like carrion birds to a battlefield.

Zephyr didn't particularly care about salvage rights—let them take what they wanted from the ship. But he was in the middle of recovering bodies that mattered, and he had no intention of dealing with complications.

He continued his work, pulling debris aside more quietly now. And there—another body. Male, young, with the same armor and symbol. Four out of five.

"Hey! Someone's over here!"

Zephyr turned to see three figures approaching—rough-looking, carrying crude weapons, wearing mismatched armor pieces clearly taken from previous "salvage operations." The leader, a scarred woman with eyes that calculated value in everything they saw, stopped when she noticed what Zephyr was doing.

"That's a body you're moving, old man."

"An observation worthy of commendation," Zephyr replied without pausing in his work.

"Dead bodies can have valuable gear." She moved closer, her companions spreading out to flank. "That armor looks custom. Expensive. We'll take it."

"You will not."

The woman's eyes narrowed. "That wasn't a request, greybeard. There's three of us, one of you, and we're not in the mood for heroes."

Zephyr straightened, the body now clear of debris. He lifted it with surprising strength for someone who looked ancient, cradling it like it weighed nothing. "I am removing this body for proper handling. You're welcome to salvage anything else in this wreckage. But not these specific dead."

"'These specific dead?'" One of the companions—a heavyset man with a cybernetic eye—laughed harshly. "You collecting bodies, old man? That's sick."

"These warriors deserve better than being stripped by scavengers."

"Warriors?" The leader's eyes gleamed. "So they were soldiers. Military grade gear, then. Even better." She raised her weapon—a battered plasma pistol that had seen better decades. "Last chance. Put down the body. Walk away. You can live."

Zephyr sighed. He really didn't have time for this.

"I'm going to give you the same courtesy," he said, his voice calm but carrying a weight that made the air feel heavier. "Walk away now. Continue your scavenging elsewhere. I won't kill you, but I will hurt you enough that you'll wish I had."

The leader actually hesitated, something in Zephyr's tone triggering survival instincts honed by years of dangerous living. But pride and greed won out.

"Take him," she ordered.

Her companions rushed forward.

What followed was less a fight than a physics demonstration. The man with the cybernetic eye swung a metal bar at Zephyr's head. The old man shifted his weight minutely—the bar whistled past, missing by a hair's breadth, and its momentum carried the man stumbling forward. Zephyr's hand shot out, touching the man's shoulder with what looked like a gentle tap.

The scavenger flew backward as if catapulted, hit the ground fifteen feet away, and didn't get up.

The second companion—wiry, fast, carrying vibroknives—came in low. Zephyr stepped into the attack rather than away from it, inside the arc of the blades, and his hand moved in a complex gesture that left tracers of golden light in the air. The knives fell from nerveless fingers as something in the attacker's nervous system simply... stopped working correctly. His legs gave out, and he collapsed, conscious but paralyzed from the neck down.

The leader had been charging, plasma pistol raised, but stopped so fast she nearly fell. Her weapon tracked Zephyr, but her hand was shaking.

"What... what are you?"

"Someone trying to do something important," Zephyr replied, still holding the body. "Someone you don't want to anger further. Take your companions. Leave. Don't come back to this section of the wreckage."

She looked at her fallen companions—one unconscious, one paralyzed, both taken down by an old man who'd done it while holding a corpse. Her survival instincts finally won.

"We're gone," she managed, backing away. "This section's yours. Whatever you're doing, it's not worth dying over."

She grabbed the unconscious one, started dragging him. The paralyzed one would recover feeling in about an hour—Zephyr had made sure of that. Not permanent damage, just a lesson.

Once they were gone, Zephyr continued his work. He brought the fourth body to his alcove, laying it with the others. Then he returned to his search.

The fifth body was the hardest to reach, trapped in a section where the ship's power systems were still active and dangerous. Emergency lighting flickered epileptically. Exposed conduits sparked and snarled. The air tasted of ozone and burning insulation.

And pinning the body—a massive beam, too heavy to move conventionally, charged with enough electricity that touching it directly would kill most beings instantly.

Zephyr studied the situation. He could use magic to move it, but this section was more active than the others. Systems were still operational, including whatever sensor arrays might still be monitoring. Using obvious magic here would be like lighting a signal flare.

But the alternative was leaving this warrior's body trapped, and that wasn't acceptable.

He made his decision.

His hands moved in precise patterns, speaking a language older than most civilizations. The First Tongue, the language that didn't describe reality but negotiated with it. His words were gentle, coaxing, asking rather than commanding. The beam's mass became negotiable. The electricity flowing through it found other paths. Gravity loosened its grip just enough.

The beam rose, slow and stately, floating on applications of force so subtle they barely registered as magic at all. Zephyr pulled the fifth body free, and—

ALARM.

Not the general emergency klaxons that had been wailing since the crash. This was specific, targeted, and came from a console ten feet away that had just flickered fully to life. A holographic display projected above it, showing the scan of this immediate area, with a bright red highlight over Zephyr's position.

UNAUTHORIZED MAGICAL SIGNATURE DETECTED
SECURITY ALERT TRANSMITTED
RESPONSE TEAM DISPATCHED

"Damn," Zephyr muttered. He'd been so careful with the amount of power, but apparently careful hadn't been careful enough. 

Distant sounds reached him—running footsteps, multiple beings, military cadence. And voices, speaking in the clipped tones of trained security forces.

He had perhaps two minutes before they arrived. The fifth body was free, but getting back to his alcove with it, gathering all five, and leaving without confrontation now seemed unlikely.

Unless he moved very quickly.

Zephyr hoisted the body and ran—or what passed for running when you were an ancient being in an old man's body. His long strides ate up distance with efficiency that had nothing to do with youth and everything to do with knowing exactly how to move through three-dimensional space with minimum wasted energy.

He reached the alcove as the first security forces rounded a corner behind him. Anunnaki guards, he noted—not the highest tier, but professional soldiers with training and gear that made the scavengers look like children playing with sticks.

"Halt! By order of the Anunnaki Security Forces, halt and identify yourself!"

Zephyr ignored them, carefully laying the fifth body with his companions. All five together now. Gerald's inner circle, the warriors who'd fought at his side through battles that had become legend.

"I said halt!" The voice was closer now, aggressive. "Turn around slowly. Keep your hands visible."

Zephyr stood, turned, and faced six Anunnaki guards in full tactical gear, weapons trained on him with the kind of steady aim that came from thousands of hours of practice. Behind them, more figures approached—a full squad, at least.

The leader—distinguished by more elaborate armor and a command presence that radiated authority—stepped forward.

"You're trespassing in a restricted salvage zone. This crash site is under Anunnaki jurisdiction until the official investigation is complete. You will surrender yourself for questioning regarding unauthorized presence and use of magical—"

His voice cut off as he finally got a good look at Zephyr's face, at the five bodies arranged with clear care in the alcove, at the protective stance the old man had taken.

Recognition flickered across the guard leader's face, replaced quickly by calculation.

"You're him," the leader said slowly. "The Sage. Zephyr."

Zephyr inclined his head slightly. "I am."

"These bodies. Why are you taking them?"

"Because they deserve better than to be catalogued as salvage."

The leader's grip on his weapon tightened. "You interfered with a crash site investigation. Used magic within restricted—"

"Are you really going to finish that sentence?" Zephyr interrupted, his tone mild but his eyes ancient and hard. "Are you really going to try to detain me over propriety violations while people are still dying in other sections of this wreckage? While your forces could be helping survivors instead of hassling an old man retrieving the honored dead?"

The guards shifted uncomfortably. Their leader's jaw worked, clearly running calculations about the political ramifications of various courses of action. Detaining the Sage would cause diplomatic headaches. Letting him go would be a breach of protocol. And nobody wanted to actually fight Zephyr—his reputation preceded him, and reputations like his weren't built on exaggeration.

"Those bodies," the leader finally said. "They're evidence. We need to document them for the investigation."

"I'll bring them to the proper authorities myself. After I've given them the rites they're owed."

"That's not acceptable—"

"Counter-offer," Zephyr interrupted. "I take these five. You pretend you never saw me. In exchange, I'll tell you that there are seventeen more survivors in the forward sections that your forces haven't reached yet. Some of them won't last another hour without help. You can waste time arguing with me, or you can save lives. Choose."

The leader's face went through several complicated expressions. "Seventeen survivors? You're certain?"

"I've been pulling people out of wreckage while your forces were still organizing. Yes, I'm certain. Forward sections, decks three through six. Some are critical. Go. Now."

For a long moment, the standoff held. Then the leader made his choice.

"You didn't see him," he said to his squad. "Understood?"

Uncertain nods.

"Forward sections, move out. Triple time." To Zephyr: "Those bodies better be treated with respect."

"They will be. You have my word."

The guards pulled back, moving toward the forward sections at a run. Zephyr waited until they were out of sight, then gathered the five bodies with gentle care, using subtle applications of force to make their weight manageable.

Then he walked out of the crash site, the five warriors cradled in his arms, the chaos of the disaster fading behind him as the desert twilight painted everything in shades of amber and shadow.

He walked until the sounds of the crash site faded to nothing, until there was only the whisper of wind across sand and the distant cry of night birds. Then he stopped, laid the bodies down in a rough circle, and stood at their center.

Five warriors. Five of Gerald's most loyal. Dead in a crash that Gerald himself had somehow survived or escaped.

Zephyr closed his eyes, reaching out with senses that transcended physical boundaries. He touched the resonance that still lingered around these dead—echoes of their loyalty, their devotion, their unwavering commitment to their leader.

And beneath that, deeper, he felt something else. A flicker. A potential. These weren't just corpses. These were vessels that had once held remarkable consciousness, warriors who'd achieved a synthesis of skill and will that happened perhaps once in a generation.

Worth saving? Perhaps. But it would require power. The kind of working that couldn't be done in a desert. The kind of ritual that demanded a place of significance.

Zephyr opened his eyes, gazing toward the horizon where he knew—though it was too far to see—that Echo Tower stood, ancient and patient, waiting for those who understood its purpose.

"You served him in life," Zephyr murmured to the bodies. "Perhaps you'll serve something greater in death. But first... the tower. First, the working."

He gathered them once more, and turned toward Echo Tower, toward possibilities that existed beyond resurrection, toward transformations that would make warriors into something else entirely.

The Five's journey wasn't over.

It was about to begin.
