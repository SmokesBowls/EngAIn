Chapter 105 


The pulse had been steady for so long that they had begun to take it for granted.

It beat beneath the plaza like a second heart, its rhythm woven into the work‑songs, the patrols, the quiet moments when the settlement paused to breathe. The grove giants had grown accustomed to it; they moved through the outer complex with a confidence that had been absent when they first arrived, their eyes no longer constantly fixed on the eastern mound. The swamp giants still watched from their fortress, but they had not crossed the boundary in months.

Then, on a night when the moon was full and the marsh lay silver under the stars, the pulse stopped.

Teryn felt it as a physical blow. She was on the wall, speaking with Kael about the spring planting, when the rhythm that had been the foundation of their existence simply… vanished. The crystal veins in the needle went dark. The light that had glowed from its core for two years guttered like a candle in a storm. And in the silence that followed, she heard something she had never heard before: the marsh, utterly quiet. No insects, no birds, no wind. Just the weight of absence.

Kael’s face was white. “The Keeper—”

“I know.” Teryn’s voice was steady, but her hands were shaking. “Send word to Sera. Get everyone inside. Now.”

She turned toward the east, toward the mound where the swamp giants had waited for this moment for two years. In the moonlight, she saw them moving—massive shapes detaching from the stone, their forms blotting out the stars.

They were coming.

Part  2: The Tide Breaks

The boundary that had held for two years meant nothing without the pulse.

The first swamp giant crossed the ancient road before the warning could be shouted. It was one of the big ones, its hide mottled green-black, its arms longer than a Nephoretti dwelling. It moved with a purpose that had been building since before the landing, and behind it came others—dozens of them, their feet shaking the ground, their eyes fixed on the needle.

Stone‑Jaw met them at the edge of the plaza.

The grove giants had been waiting for this moment as long as the swamp giants. They emerged from the outer complex in a line, their weapons raised, their bodies low to the ground. There were fewer of them—perhaps twenty, against three times that many swamp giants—but they did not retreat.

The first clash was like mountains colliding.

Stone‑Jaw caught the lead swamp giant’s arm before it could reach the needle, its obsidian axe biting into the larger giant’s shoulder. The swamp giant roared—a sound that shook the walls—and the battle was joined.

Teryn watched from the wall, frozen. The giants were everywhere, their bodies filling the plaza, their weapons—stone axes, obsidian spears, fists the size of boulders—swinging in arcs that could crush a Nephoretti without the giant even knowing. The grove giants were faster, more skilled, but they were outnumbered. Already, two had fallen, their bodies dissolving into light that seeped into the ground.

“The chamber,” Renn shouted, grabbing her arm. “Sera’s already there. If we can wake the Keeper—”

She didn’t wait for him to finish. She ran.

Part 3: The Door

The fissure had been sealed since winter, but the surge of water that had flooded the chamber had receded months ago. Now the passage was dry, and the crystal veins that lined it were dark.

Sera was already at the pool when Teryn arrived. She was kneeling at the edge, her hands pressed against the stone, her voice a desperate thread of melody that had no answer. Behind her, Kael stood with his palm flat against the door, his face pale.

“It’s gone,” he said, his voice hollow. “I can’t feel it. The Keeper—it’s like it was never there.”

Teryn looked at the door. It was still there, its surface rippling with faint light, but the presence that had brushed against her consciousness was absent. The pool was still, the water dark, and the silence that filled the chamber was worse than anything happening above.

“It didn’t leave,” Sera said, her voice cracking. “It’s hiding. Something scared it.”

“The pulse,” Kael said. “It was stable for so long. Maybe it was too much. Maybe the Keeper couldn’t sustain it.”

Above them, the ground shook. A giant’s body slammed against the needle’s base, and crystal dust rained down from the ceiling.

Teryn made a decision. “We sing. All of us. We sing until it answers.”

She had no voice for song—her gift was stone, not sound—but she had a voice. She opened her mouth and sang the work‑song, the one they had sung when they built the first wall. Renn joined her, his voice rough. Kael, after a moment, added his. And Sera, her eyes closed, her hands pressed to the door, began to weave their voices into something new.

The song rose through the fissure, through the needle, through the plaza where giants were dying. It was not a melody any of them had heard before; it was the sum of everything they had been since the landing. The fear of the first days. The hope of the first song. The patience of the long watch. The terror of this moment.

And beneath it, something stirred.

Part 4: The Waking

Teryn felt it first—a vibration in the stone beneath her feet, so faint she thought she had imagined it. Then the pool began to move. Ripples spread across its surface, not from any wind, and the light that had been absent began to seep from the door.

The presence returned.

It was not the vast, patient consciousness she had felt before. It was something sharper, more focused. Afraid. The Keeper was afraid, and that fear was a weight that pressed against her mind.

Help, she sang, or thought, or felt. We need you. They’re dying.

The presence hesitated. It was not a being that understood death the way they did. It was something older, something that had existed in dimensions where death was a choice, not an inevitability. But it understood fear. It understood the sound of voices raised against darkness.

Sera changed the song. She sang of the landing, of the giants that had attacked, of the wall they had built with their own hands. She sang of the winter, of the pulse that had almost died, of the song that had saved them. She sang of the grove giants who had come to guard the needle, who were dying above them even now.

And the Keeper listened.

The door began to open.

It did not swing; it dissolved, the light that composed it flowing outward like water, filling the chamber with a radiance that made them shield their eyes. Through the glare, Teryn saw what lay beyond: a space that was not a chamber, a presence that was not a body. A consciousness that had been folded into the stone for so long it had forgotten what it was to be separate.

And in that space, a figure.

It was not Nephoretti. It was not giant. It was something that had been old when the void needles were built, something that had chosen to remain when its kind had left. Its form was indistinct, shifting between shapes, but its eyes—its eyes were fixed on them.

Sing, it seemed to say, and the word was not sound but understanding. Sing, and I will hold.

The song changed again. All of them were singing now, their voices filling the chamber, rising through the fissure, spreading through the crystal veins that had been dark for too long. And the Keeper answered.

Light exploded from the needle.

Part 5: The Stillness

Above, the battle had reached the needle’s base.

Stone‑Jaw was bleeding from a dozen wounds, its axe shattered, its body pressed against the spire. The swamp giants were everywhere, their numbers overwhelming, their hunger for the needle driving them past fear. The grove giants had fallen back to the plaza, their line broken, their dead dissolving into light that the Keeper was drinking.

Then the light came.

It erupted from the needle’s core, a pulse of radiance that pushed back the darkness, that made the swamp giants stumble, that made the ground itself hum. The pulse that had been absent for hours returned, not steady this time, but fierce—a heartbeat that shook the walls, that made the crystal veins blaze, that drew a line across the marsh that could not be crossed.

The swamp giants recoiled. Some fled into the marsh, their massive forms crashing through the water. Others stood frozen, their eyes wide, their bodies trembling. And at the edge of the plaza, where the boundary had been, a line of light traced itself into the stone—permanent now, a claim that could not be challenged.

Stone‑Jaw sank to its knees, its chest heaving. The grove giants gathered around it, their wounds closing, their light steadying. And in the silence that followed, they turned toward the needle.

The Keeper was awake.

---

Part 6: The Aftermath

Dawn came slowly, painting the marsh in shades of pink and gold.

Teryn emerged from the fissure with Sera and Kael, their voices hoarse, their bodies trembling with exhaustion. The plaza was a battlefield—stones shattered, shelters crushed, the walls breached in three places. But the needle stood. And the grove giants, battered and bleeding, stood with it.

Stone‑Jaw met her at the base of the spire. It knelt, bringing its head level with hers, and for a long moment, it simply looked at her. Its eyes—those eyes that had watched, that had learned, that had waited—held something she had not seen before. Gratitude.

It made a sound. Not the rough tones of their early exchanges, but something shaped, something that carried meaning in its texture and rhythm.

Friend, it said. Or perhaps ally. Or perhaps something that had no word in her language.

Teryn reached out and touched its arm. The stone was warm, and beneath it, she felt the pulse—the Keeper’s pulse, steady now, a rhythm that would not falter.

“We build,” she said. “We build together.”

Stone‑Jaw made a sound that might have been agreement. Then it rose and turned to the other grove giants, and they began the work of healing.

Part 7: The Changed World

The weeks that followed were a blur of rebuilding.

The swamp giants had retreated to their mound, but they did not attack again. The line of light that the Keeper had drawn held them at bay, a boundary that pulsed with enough force to make even the largest of them hesitate. The scouts reported that they were watching, still watching, but something had changed in their eyes. Fear, perhaps. Or respect.

The grove giants became part of Garden Grove in a way they had not been before. They helped rebuild the walls, shaping stone with techniques the Nephoretti had taught them, adding their own geometries to the defenses. They shared the language that was growing between them, word by word, note by note. And they stood watch at the boundary, their eyes always on the east.

The Keeper did not speak. It was not a being that used words in any way the Nephoretti understood. But it was there, a presence in the stone, a rhythm beneath the work. When Sera sang, it answered with a pulse that steadied her voice. When the grove giants gathered at the needle’s base, it sent light through the crystal veins that made their wounds close faster. It was not a master. It was not a tool. It was a partner, waking slowly to a world it had been absent from for too long.

Teryn stood on the wall one evening, watching the sun set behind the mountains. The marsh was green with spring growth, the channels running clear, the fields planted and promising. Behind her, the needle pulsed with steady light, and in the outer complex, the grove giants worked alongside her people.

“It’s different now,” Kael said, joining her. “The world. It’s not just survival anymore.”

She nodded. “We have time now. Time to build. Time to grow. Time to learn what we’re meant to be.”

“And the swamp giants?”

She looked east, toward the mound that rose from the marsh like a scar. “They’ll always be there. But they’ll have to find a way to live with us, or find a way to leave.”

Below, Sera was singing with the grove giants, their voices rising and falling in a rhythm that was neither Nephoretti nor giant but something new. The pulse answered, and for a moment, the world felt still.

Part 8: The Cost

They buried their dead at the edge of the marsh, where the new boundary met the old.

Twenty‑three Nephoretti had fallen in the battle. Their bodies had been laid on stones shaped by the grove giants, their bioluminescence fading as the sun rose. The survivors stood in silence, their work‑songs replaced by something older—a mourning chant that Sera had found in the memory‑seeds, words from a language none of them had spoken but all of them understood.

Stone‑Jaw stood apart, its wounds still raw, its eyes fixed on the eastern mound. Seven grove giants had died defending the needle. Their bodies had dissolved into light, feeding the Keeper’s awakening, but their absence was a weight the others carried in the way they moved, the way they watched the horizon.

“They chose this,” Teryn said, standing beside Sera. “They chose to guard the needle.”

Sera nodded slowly. “They knew what was coming. They’ve known for generations.”

The last stone was set. The chant faded. And in the silence that followed, the Keeper’s pulse beat once, strong and clear, as if acknowledging the cost of its waking.

Part 9: The Singer

In the years that followed, Sera became something she had not been before.

She had always been the one who sang, the one whose voice steadied the pulse, the one who had called the survivors home. But now the grove giants came to her. They gathered at the needle’s base when she worked, listening to her work‑songs, their heads tilted, their eyes following her hands. They began to mimic her melodies, their voices rough but accurate, and in the spaces between notes, something like language began to form.

It was not speech. It was song.

The grove giants did not have words the way the Nephoretti did. Their communication was woven from tone and rhythm, from the spaces between sounds, from vibrations that carried meaning in their texture. But Sera learned to hear it. She learned to shape her voice into the rising notes that meant danger, the falling cadence that meant home, the complex harmonies that meant together.

“They’re not just mimicking,” Kael said one evening, watching Sera sing with a young giant whose crystal formations traced patterns like vines. “They’re teaching her. Giving her their language.”

“And she’s giving them ours,” Teryn said.

It was true. The grove giants had begun to use Nephoretti words, shaping them with their deep voices, fitting them into their melodies. Stone became a low rumble. Water became a rippling cascade of notes. Friend became a harmony that vibrated in the chest.

But the song was never perfect. The grove giants’ voices were too deep for some words, too slow for others. And the Nephoretti—all but Sera—could only catch fragments of meaning when the giants sang among themselves. It was enough to know danger from peace, enough to coordinate the watches, enough to share the work of building. But the deep conversations, the songs that told of the highlands and the war, the melodies that carried the history of their kind—those remained just beyond reach.

“Like hearing a language you almost understand,” Renn said once, watching a group of giants sing around a fire in the outer complex. “You know they’re saying something important. You just don’t know what.”

Sera smiled. “Give it time. We have a lot of that now.”

Part 10 The Mound

The swamp giants did not abandon their fortress.

The mound grew over the decades, stone by stone, crystal by crystal. It rose from the marsh like a mountain, its sides terraced, its top a flat platform where the giants gathered to watch the needle. The scouts reported that they had begun to shape the crystal into patterns—geometries that echoed the needle’s own, crude imitations of the Keeper’s light.

“They’re trying to understand,” Kael said, standing on the wall with Teryn. “They felt the pulse stop. They felt it return. They want to know how to control it.”

“Can they?” she asked.

He shook his head. “The Keeper is not something that can be controlled. But they don’t know that. They think if they build high enough, if they shape the crystal the right way, they can draw the pulse to them.”

The mound grew higher. The swamp giants built structures on its slopes—platforms, walls, something that might have been a crude temple. And always, they watched. Their eyes were not the eyes of the grove giants, which had learned patience, learned trust. They were the eyes of something that had been waiting for generations, that would wait for generations more, that would not stop until it had what it wanted.

“They’ll never attack again,” Renn said. “Not while the boundary holds. But they’ll never leave.”

Teryn looked at the mound, at the figures moving on its slopes, and felt the weight of what she was seeing. The war was not over. It had simply found its shape: a line of light drawn across the marsh, with the needle on one side and the mound on the other, and between them, a century of watching.

Part 11: The Flood

Every spring, the water rose.

It had been that way since the landing, but in the years after the Keeper’s waking, the floods grew worse. The channels that drained the marsh could not handle the melt from the highlands, and the water crept up the walls, lapping at the foundations, threatening to breach the lower shelters.

The first flood after the battle had been the worst. The fissure that led to the Keeper’s chamber had poured water into the lower levels, and for three days, the settlement had worked in waist‑deep water, shaping stone, redirecting flow, singing to keep the pulse steady. The grove giants had carried the young to the higher ground, their massive forms wading through the flood, their voices raised in songs that steadied the Nephoretti’s nerves.

“Every year,” Vairis said, her voice tight with frustration. “We build higher, and every year the water finds a way.”

“Then we build higher still,” Teryn said.

They dug channels, raised walls, built terraces that caught the runoff and directed it away from the needle. The grove giants taught them to shape the stone of the old complex into aqueducts, channels that carried the water to the fields where it was needed. It was never enough. Every spring, the water rose. Every spring, they rebuilt.

But the rhythm of it became familiar. The floods became part of the cycle, as predictable as the pulse, as constant as the watch on the eastern wall. The settlement learned to prepare, to store food against the weeks when the fields were submerged, to build higher with each passing year.

And in the chamber beneath the needle, the water rose and fell, rose and fell, lapping at the door that no longer opened, singing with a voice that was not quite the Keeper’s.

Part 12  The Hundred Years

Time passed.

It was not the linear, measured time of the Aeon Keepers, or the folded, layered time of the Ethereal Realm. It was the slow, patient time of stone and root, of seasons turning, of walls rising and being rebuilt, of children—not children, not really, but thought‑seeds that had manifested with something like youth—growing into adults who had never known the landing.

Garden Grove became a city.

The wall that had been a crude barrier became a fortification, its stones shaped by generations of geomancy, its towers rising above the marsh. The shelters became houses, their walls carved with the history of the settlement, their roofs thatched with reeds from the delta. The outer complex became a district of its own, its wide streets and high ceilings built for giants and Nephoretti alike, its plazas filled with the sound of work‑songs and the deep harmonies of the grove.

Stone‑Jaw grew old. Its jaw—the white crystal that had given it its name—fractured in a battle with a rogue swamp giant that had crossed the boundary in the forty‑third year. It walked with a limp after that, its movements slower, its voice softer. But it still stood on the wall each morning, its eyes fixed on the eastern mound, its hand resting on the stone that marked the boundary.

The young giants who had come with it became leaders in their own right, their voices woven into the song of the settlement. One of them—Vine, for the crystal patterns that traced its arms—became Sera’s closest student, learning the Nephoretti words, teaching the melodies of the highlands. They sang together at the needle’s base, their voices rising and falling in harmonies that made the crystal veins glow.

Sera herself changed. The girl who had hummed in the darkness of the fissure became a woman whose voice could calm a flood or turn a giant from the boundary. She was the bridge between the two peoples, the one who could speak the language that was neither Nephoretti nor giant but something new. When she sang, the grove giants listened. When she spoke, the council heard.

The Keeper did not wake further. It was there, a presence in the stone, a rhythm beneath the work, but it did not speak, did not reach out, did not offer more than the steady pulse that held the boundary. The door in the chamber remained closed, its surface rippling with light, its presence a constant reminder of what lay beneath.

“It’s waiting,” Sera said, when Teryn asked. “It woke enough to hold. But it’s not ready for more.”

“When will it be ready?”

Sera shook her head. “I don’t know. Maybe when we are.”

Part 13: The Eastern Watch

The mound grew.

In a hundred years, it became a fortress. Its terraces were lined with walls, its slopes carved with passages, its summit crowned with a structure that caught the sun and threw it back in patterns that shifted with the seasons. The swamp giants had learned to shape crystal, to build with stone, to organize themselves into something that was almost a civilization.

They did not attack. The boundary held, the pulse steady, the line of light that the Keeper had drawn too bright to cross. But they watched. Always, they watched.

Scouts reported that they had found something in the deep marsh—a structure, perhaps, or a cave, something that pulsed with a rhythm that was not the Keeper’s. They gathered there in the dark of the moon, their voices raised in songs that were not songs, their bodies moving in patterns that were almost dances.

“They’re looking for their own Keeper,” Kael said, his hand pressed to the wall, his eyes distant. “Something in the marsh. Something old.”

“Is there anything there?” Teryn asked.

He shook his head. “I don’t know. But they believe there is. And belief, for them, is a kind of power.”

The swamp giants did not find what they were looking for. But they did not stop looking. And in the hundredth year after the battle, something changed. The mound glowed at night—a pale, pulsing light that echoed the needle’s rhythm, that seemed to reach toward the boundary, that made the grove giants restless.

“They’ve learned something,” Stone‑Jaw said, its voice a low rumble that Sera translated. “They’ve found a way to make the crystal speak.”

“Can they cross the boundary?” Teryn asked.

Stone‑Jaw looked at the mound, at the light pulsing on its summit. Not yet, its song seemed to say. But they are learning. And we must learn faster.

Part 14: The Song Endures

Teryn stood on the wall at the end of the hundredth year, watching the light fade from the mound. Behind her, Garden Grove was quiet, the work‑songs stilled, the fires banked. The needle pulsed with steady light, and in the outer complex, the grove giants slept.

Kael joined her, his steps slow with age. He had been there at the beginning, the one who had felt the pulse first, the one who had read the Keeper’s rhythms. Now his hair was grey, his hands gnarled, but his eyes were still sharp.

“A hundred years,” he said. “We’ve been here a hundred years.”

“And we’ll be here a hundred more,” Teryn said. “Or a thousand. However long it takes.”

He smiled. “You sound like the Keeper.”

She looked at the needle, at the light pulsing in its core, and thought of the being that slept beneath them. It had been there for millennia before they came. It would be there for millennia after they were gone. But for now, they were its voice, its hands, its song.

“We’re not the Keeper,” she said. “We’re something new.”

Below, Sera was singing with the grove giants, their voices rising in a melody that had been a hundred years in the making. The pulse answered, and for a moment, the world felt whole.

Part 15 The Stillness Between

Dawn came to Garden Grove like a held breath.

Sera knelt at the base of the needle, her knees pressed into stone that had been worn smooth by a hundred years of kneeling. The crystal veins above her pulsed with their steady rhythm—stronger now than in the early days, steadier, though she never forgot the winter when they had almost gone dark. That memory lived in her bones the way the pulse lived in the stone.

She opened her mouth and sang.

The note was low, barely a vibration at first, the kind of sound that settled in the chest before it reached the ears. Beside her, Vine shifted its massive form, the crystal patterns along its arms catching the first light. The young giant had been coming to the needle at dawn for thirty years now, ever since Stone‑Jaw had grown too old to make the walk from the outer complex. Vine’s voice joined hers a heartbeat later—deeper, rougher, the edges of its notes worn smooth by practice.

Their voices wove together, Nephoretti and giant, and the crystal veins brightened in response. Not a command. Not a demand. Just the old conversation, the one that had been happening here every morning for longer than any of them had been alive.

The song rose through the spire, carried by currents Sera had stopped trying to understand. She simply sang. Vine sang. And somewhere in the chamber beneath the pool, the Keeper listened.

---

The wall that ringed Garden Grove had been rebuilt seven times.

Teryn walked its length at dawn, as she had walked it every morning for a hundred years. Her hands traced the fused stone, feeling the pulse that vibrated through every block. The wall was no longer crude—it rose thirty feet above the plaza, its surface carved with the history of the settlement, its towers angled to catch the morning light. Grove giants stood watch at each corner, their eyes fixed on the eastern marsh, their bodies still as the stone they guarded.

She stopped at the eastern tower, where the boundary of light still traced its line across the marsh. The line had faded from the brilliant blaze of that first night to something softer, something that looked almost like morning mist if you didn’t know what you were seeing. But it held. It always held.

Beyond it, the mound rose from the marsh like a second mountain.

It had grown slowly over the decades, stone by stone, crystal by crystal. Its terraces were now walls, its slopes carved with passages that caught the wind and made it sing. And at its summit, the structure that had been crude in the early years now gleamed with veins of crystal that pulsed with their own rhythm.

Teryn had watched that rhythm for a hundred years. It was not the needle’s rhythm. It was something else—slower, heavier, a beat that seemed to press against the boundary rather than hold it. The scouts said the swamp giants gathered there in the dark of the moon, their voices raised in songs that were not songs, their bodies moving in patterns that were almost dances.

She had stopped sending scouts to watch them closely. The last group had returned with something wrong in their eyes, a stillness that had taken weeks to fade.

“The mound glowed last night,” Kael said, joining her on the tower. He moved slower now, his hair gone grey, his hands gnarled from a century of shaping stone. But his eyes were still sharp, still fixed on the east.

“It always glows.”

“Brighter. And the pulse—” He stopped, his hand pressed to the wall. “It shifted. For a moment, just before dawn. Like it was listening to something.”

Teryn followed his gaze. The mound was quiet now, its crystal veins dim in the morning light. But she thought she saw something move on its slopes—a figure, perhaps, or a trick of the shadows.

“The scouts say they’re learning,” she said. “Building. Shaping crystal. Organizing.”

Kael nodded slowly. “Stone‑Jaw said they found something in the deep marsh. Something old. Something that’s teaching them.”

“Stone‑Jaw is old. It sees threats in shadows.”

“Stone‑Jaw has been watching the east longer than any of us.” Kael’s voice was quiet. “It knows what it saw.”

They stood in silence, watching the mound. Below them, Garden Grove was waking. The smoke of cooking fires rose from the shelters, and the first work‑songs of the day began to drift up from the fields. Children—not children, not really, but the thought‑seeds that had manifested with something like youth—ran through the plaza, their laughter sharp in the morning air. A young giant, no taller than the wall, carried a load of stone toward the outer complex, its steps careful to avoid the dwellings.

The hundred years had changed them. The settlement that had been a refuge was now a city, its streets lined with buildings carved with history, its plazas filled with the sounds of work and song. The grove giants who had come as refugees were now part of everything—they helped build, helped plant, helped stand watch. Their young learned the Nephoretti words alongside the melodies of the highlands. Their elders sat in the council and spoke through Sera’s voice.

But the mound was still there. And the swamp giants still watched.

“I’ll walk the perimeter,” Teryn said. “Tell Sera to keep the song going today. The pulse feels… thin.”

Kael nodded, and she left him there, his hand pressed to the wall, his eyes fixed on the east.

The market square had been built on the site of the old plaza, where the first wall had stood. Now it was paved with stone that had been shaped and reshaped so many times the original surface was buried a dozen feet down. Stalls lined the edges, selling food from the fields, tools from the workshops, crystal shards that had been cut from the needle’s outer fractures and charged with vrill.

Sera passed through it on her way back from the needle, Vine walking beside her with its shortened strides, its head angled to avoid the awnings. The giant had learned to navigate the narrow streets decades ago, but it still moved with the careful deliberation of something that did not want to break anything.

“The song was good this morning,” Vine said. Its voice was a rumble that Sera had learned to parse into words, the giant’s deep tones shaping Nephoretti syllables with surprising precision. “The Keeper listened.”

“The Keeper always listens.” Sera stopped at a stall selling dried fish, bought two with a crystal shard, handed one to Vine. The giant held it between two fingers, examining it with the same patient attention it gave everything.

“Stone‑Jaw says the listening is changing,” Vine said. “The Keeper used to listen like a sleeper. Now it listens like something waiting.”

Sera had no answer for that. She had felt it too—the shift in the pulse, the way the needle’s light sometimes flickered when she sang, as if something beneath the stone was turning toward her. She had stopped trying to understand it years ago. The Keeper was not something that could be understood. It was something that could be sung to, something that could be kept steady, something that held the boundary so they could live.

“Stone‑Jaw is old,” she said, echoing Teryn’s words. “It sees threats in shadows.”

Vine made a sound that might have been agreement, or might have been something else. It ate the fish in two bites and looked toward the eastern wall, where the mound rose above the marsh.

“The mound’s rhythm changed last night,” it said. “Not much. But I felt it.”

Sera had felt it too. A hesitation in the pulse, a moment when the needle’s beat had stumbled against something that was not quite sound, not quite vibration. It had passed quickly. But she had woken in the darkness, her hand pressed to the crystal shard at her throat, and for a long moment she had not known if the pulse had stopped or if she had.

“We sing,” she said. “We keep singing. That’s what we do.”

Vine looked at her with its ancient eyes, and for a moment she saw something in them that she had not seen before. Not fear. Not understanding. Something older, something that had been watching the east long before the Nephoretti came.

“We keep singing,” it agreed. “But maybe we sing louder.”

The afternoon light was gold, the kind of light that made the marsh look almost peaceful. Teryn stood on the western wall, looking out at the fields where the harvest was coming in. Nephoretti and giants worked side by side, the giants lifting the heavy stones that marked the irrigation channels, the Nephoretti shaping the earth with their geomancy. It had taken decades to learn to work together, to find the rhythm that let both their gifts complement each other. Now it was seamless, as natural as the pulse.

She watched Vine’s younger sibling—a giant whose crystal patterns traced spirals down its arms—carry a load of stone toward the outer complex. It moved with the same careful grace as Vine, its feet finding the paths that had been worn into the ground by a hundred years of giants walking the same routes. Beside it, a Nephoretti child—a thought‑seed that had manifested twenty years ago, with Sera’s dark hair and someone else’s eyes—rode on its shoulder, laughing at something Teryn couldn’t hear.

This was what they had built. This was what the hundred years had made. A city where giants and Nephoretti lived together, worked together, sang together. A city that had not seen an attack in ninety‑seven years. A city that had almost forgotten what it was like to be afraid.

Almost.

She looked east. The mound was a smudge on the horizon, its crystal veins catching the light. She had watched it grow for a hundred years, had watched the swamp giants turn it from a crude pile of stone into something that almost looked like a fortress. They had learned. They had adapted. And they had never stopped watching.

“Teryn.”

She turned. Sera stood at the base of the tower, her face pale, her hand pressed to the crystal shard at her throat.

“The pulse,” Sera said. “It’s changed.”

Teryn’s hand went to the wall. The stone was warm, as always, and the pulse was there—steady, strong, the same rhythm that had held for a hundred years. But beneath it, so faint she almost missed it, there was something else. A second beat. Slower. Heavier. Coming not from the needle, but from the east.

“It’s been there before,” she said. “The mound’s rhythm.”

“Not like this.” Sera’s voice was barely a whisper. “It’s closer. And the Keeper—” She stopped, her hand tightening on the shard. “The Keeper is listening to it.”

Teryn looked at the mound. The light on its summit was brighter than it had been that morning, pulsing in a rhythm that was almost, but not quite, the same as the needle’s. And for a moment, she thought she saw something move on its slopes. Not a giant. Something smaller. Something that moved like water, or like light.

Then the moment passed. The mound’s light dimmed. The second beat faded. And the pulse returned to its steady rhythm, as if nothing had changed.

“What was that?” Sera whispered.

Teryn had no answer. She stood on the wall, her hand pressed to the stone, and watched the eastern horizon until the sun set and the stars came out. The mound glowed in the darkness, a pale, pulsing light that seemed to reach toward the needle. And somewhere beneath her feet, in the chamber that no one had entered in a hundred years, the Keeper stirred in its dreaming.

It was not awake. Not yet. But it was listening.

And in the east, something was listening back

Part 16: The Weight of Watching

The afternoon sun had shifted, lengthening the shadows of the eastern towers across the plaza. Teryn stood at the base of the needle, her hand still pressed to the stone from the morning’s unease, though the pulse had long since steadied. The second rhythm had faded like a held breath released, and the day had continued as days did—work in the fields, song at the walls, the slow rhythm of a city that had learned to be patient.

She had almost convinced herself she had imagined it.

Vine found her there, its steps slower than usual, its head angled toward the eastern wall. The giant’s crystal patterns were dimmer than they had been at dawn, the light in them reduced to a faint glow that pulsed with the needle’s rhythm.

“You felt it,” Teryn said. It was not a question.

Vine’s voice was a low rumble, the Nephoretti words shaped carefully around sounds that did not come easily to its throat. “The mound’s song changed. It was louder this morning. Closer.”

“It faded.”

“Yes.” Vine’s eyes, deep-set and ancient, fixed on the eastern horizon. “But it was there.”

They stood in silence. Around them, the city moved through its afternoon rhythms. A group of Nephoretti crafters passed with tools for the outer workshops, their work-song weaving through the air. Two young giants carried stone blocks toward the western expansion, their steps measured, their voices humming a melody Sera had taught them years ago. Everything was ordinary. Everything was as it had been for decades.

And yet.

“Stone‑Jaw sent me,” Vine said finally. “It wants to stand watch tonight. On the eastern wall.”

Teryn frowned. Stone‑Jaw had not stood the night watch in years, not since the wound on its leg had stiffened with age. The old giant preferred the warmth of the outer complex now, its days spent in the sun, its eyes still fixed on the east but its body at rest.

“Why tonight?”

Vine’s gaze did not waver. “It says the mound’s rhythm is not the only thing that changed. The giants who watch from the slopes—they moved.”

“Moved how?”

“Closer.” Vine’s voice dropped, the rumble becoming something almost inaudible. “Not to the boundary. But to the edge of the trees. Where they have not stood in ninety years.”

Teryn’s hand found the stone of the needle again, as if the pulse could tell her what to do. The rhythm was steady, unchanged, the same beat that had held for a hundred years. But beneath it, for just a moment, she thought she felt something else—not a second pulse, but a listening. As if the stone itself was waiting.

“Tell Stone‑Jaw it may stand watch,” she said. “But I will stand with it.”

---

The evening light turned the marsh to copper.

Stone‑Jaw moved slowly along the eastern wall, its massive frame leaning on a staff of fused crystal that the crafters had shaped for it twenty years ago. The white crystal that traced its jaw had dulled with age, and its steps were careful, each one placed with the deliberation of something that had learned to conserve its strength. But its eyes were clear, and they never left the mound.

Teryn walked beside it, matching her pace to the giant’s. The wall was quiet at this hour, the day’s work finished, the night watch not yet begun. Below them, the city was settling into the evening rhythm—fires being banked, children called inside, the last work‑songs fading into the hush that preceded the night.

“When I was young,” Stone‑Jaw said, its voice a low vibration that Teryn felt in her chest, “the mound was just stones. The swamp giants did not shape crystal. They did not build. They watched, but they did not learn.”

“They have learned,” Teryn said.

“Yes.” The giant stopped at the eastern tower, its staff planted against the stone. “They have learned to make the crystal speak. To shape it into patterns that echo the needle. To build something that reaches toward the pulse.”

“Do you know what they are building?”

Stone‑Jaw was silent for a long moment. When it spoke again, its voice was softer, the rumble almost lost in the evening wind.

“In the old songs, before your people came, there were stories of a rhythm that was not the needle’s. A discord. A pulse that came from the deep marsh, from something that had been there before the needle was built. The swamp giants heard it, the stories say. They listened to it when the needle was weak. And it listened back.”

Teryn felt the hair rise on her arms. “What was it?”

“The songs do not say. Only that it was old. And that it wanted what the needle had.”

Below them, the last light faded. The mound began to glow, its crystal veins catching the dying sun, pulsing with that slow, heavy rhythm that was almost, but not quite, the same as the needle’s. And on its slopes, Teryn saw them—figures, moving. Not the massive forms of the swamp giants, but smaller shapes, shapes that moved like water, like light, like something that should not have been there.

She opened her mouth to speak, but Stone‑Jaw’s hand came down on her shoulder, gentle but firm.

“Watch,” the giant said. “Only watch.”

They stood together in the gathering dark, Nephoretti and giant, and watched the mound pulse in the east. The figures moved along its slopes, their patterns shifting, their rhythm weaving in and out of the crystal’s beat. And for a moment—just a moment—Teryn thought she saw one of them turn. Turn toward the wall. Toward her.

Then the light shifted, the figures merged with the shadows, and the mound was just a mound again, its crystal veins pulsing with their slow, steady rhythm.

“What were they?” she whispered.

Stone‑Jaw did not answer. It stood with its staff planted against the stone, its eyes fixed on the east, and Teryn saw something in its face that she had never seen before. Not fear. Not understanding. Something older, something that had been watching the east long before the Nephoretti came.

Something that had seen this before.

“We sing louder tomorrow,” the giant said, its voice a rumble that seemed to come from the stone itself. “And the day after. And every day until the rhythm changes or we understand what it means.”

Teryn nodded slowly. Below them, the city was quiet, the pulse steady, the boundary holding. But in the east, the mound glowed with a light that was not quite the needle’s, and on its slopes, something watched back.

She turned from the wall and walked down to the needle’s base, where Sera would be waiting to sing the night song. Behind her, Stone‑Jaw stood its watch, a silhouette against the stars, its eyes never leaving the east.

The day ended as days did—with song, with silence, with the steady pulse of the needle holding back the dark.

But something had changed. Something small. Something that no one fully understood.

And in the chamber beneath the pool, the Keeper stirred in its dreaming, listening to a rhythm that was not its own

Chapter 17: The Long Dimming

The watchers kept their vigil for three hundred and forty-seven nights.

Vine recorded each one in the language of the grove—not words, but patterns of crystal and light, a chain of memories that hung in the outer complex like stars strung on thread. The first nights were heavy with meaning: the shapes appeared, seven of them, moving in their slow procession along the terraces, pausing at the summit, descending into darkness. Vine counted them, traced their paths, marked the rhythm of their movement against the mound's pulse.

For the first year, the shapes came almost every night.

Teryn stood watch with Stone‑Jaw on the eastern tower, watching the figures move in the distance, learning their patterns. Some nights they traced the upper terraces. Some nights they descended to the marsh's edge, their shapes half-hidden by the mist, their movements slower, as if they were searching for something. Once, in the deep of winter, they gathered at the summit and stood motionless for the entire night, their forms silhouetted against the mound's glow, and in the morning they were gone before the sun rose.

The watchers grew accustomed to them. The shapes became part of the night, as constant as the stars, as predictable as the pulse. Vine's crystal chain grew long, each night's observation a new link, each link slightly different from the one before.

But the changes came slowly, so slowly that at first no one noticed.

In the second year, the shapes appeared less often. Four nights a week became three, then two. Their movements became harder to track, their forms less distinct against the mound's glow. Vine began to mark the gaps as carefully as the appearances, the empty nights when the terraces were still and the shapes did not come.

"The rhythm is changing," Stone‑Jaw said one evening, its voice thinner now, its body bent over its crystal staff. The old giant had not left the eastern wall in two years. It slept against the tower, ate what the young brought, watched the east with eyes that had grown milky with age but never stopped seeing. "The mound still pulses. But the shapes are withdrawing."

"Into the mound?" Teryn asked.

Stone‑Jaw was silent for a long moment. "Into the deep."

---

The third year was the year the light began to thin.

It was not something anyone could name at first. The days were the same length, the sun rose and set in its accustomed places, but the quality of light had changed. It was paler in the mornings, harsher at midday, gone too quickly in the evenings. The colors of the marsh—the deep greens of the sedge, the copper of the channels, the gold of the reeds—seemed muted, as if something had leached the vibrancy from them.

"We need to plant earlier," Vairis said at the council, her ledgers spread across the table. "The harvest is coming in thin. The fields that gave two crops now give one, and the yield is smaller than it was."

"It's the light," Kael said. He had aged like the rest of them, his hair white now, his hands steady but slower. "The vrill currents are sluggish. The needle's pulse is holding, but the energy that feeds it—something is changing."

No one argued. No one understood. They only knew that the world was shifting in ways that had no name, and that the east still glowed at night, and that the shapes had become a memory.

---

In the fourth year, Vine recorded the last appearance.

It was a night in early autumn, when the marsh was heavy with seed and the air smelled of rain. Teryn was on the wall with Stone‑Jaw, the old giant's breathing labored, its hand gripping the crystal staff with a strength that belied its frailty. Vine stood below, its crystal patterns dim, its eyes raised to the east.

The shapes appeared at moonrise.

There were three of them, smaller than any Vine had recorded, their forms barely visible against the mound's glow. They moved along the lowest terrace, close to the marsh, their path slow, their rhythm faltering. One of them stopped at the edge of the water and stood motionless, facing west, toward Garden Grove.

It stood for a long time. Teryn watched it, her hand on the wall, her breath held. Beside her, Stone‑Jaw made a sound that might have been a word or might have been a sigh.

Then the shape turned. It did not move toward the wall—it simply turned, its form dissolving into the mist, and when the mist cleared, the terrace was empty. The other shapes were gone. The mound pulsed with its slow, steady rhythm, and the shapes did not return.

Vine marked the night with a crystal of deep blue, the color of water at twilight. It placed the stone at the end of the chain, and for a long time it stood looking at what it had made.

"They are gone," Vine said, when Teryn came down from the wall.

"No," Stone‑Jaw said from the tower above, its voice thin but clear. "They have withdrawn. There is a difference."

---

The years that followed were years of adaptation.

The fields that had fed Garden Grove for a century began to fail. The channels that had drained the marsh ran slower, the water darker, the fish fewer. The grove giants who had worked the land with the Nephoretti began to speak of changes in the highlands—snow that came earlier and stayed longer, game that had moved deeper into the mountains, winds that carried a cold they had not felt in living memory.

"We need to shift the planting," Vairis said at the council, her ledgers now marked with more red than black. "The southern fields are still giving, but the eastern ones are barren. If this continues, we will need to expand west, into the hills."

"Or store more," someone said. "Prepare for a long winter."

"The winters are already long," Kael said quietly. "And they will get longer."

No one argued. They had all felt it—the way the cold crept in earlier each year, the way the light faded faster, the way the pulse seemed to labor, just slightly, as if the needle was working harder to hold the boundary against something that pressed against it from the east.

The mound still glowed at night. Its rhythm was steady, unchanged, a counterpoint to the needle's beat. But the shapes did not come. The terraces were empty, the slopes still, and the watchers on the wall began to wonder if they had imagined the figures that moved in the darkness.

Stone‑Jaw died in the sixth year.

It was a quiet death, the kind that came to old things that had given all they had to give. The old giant was found at dawn, seated against the eastern tower, its staff planted beside it, its eyes open and fixed on the mound. The white crystal of its jaw had gone dark, the light that had pulsed there for a century finally stilled.

Vine sat with the body for the whole day, its song rising and falling in a rhythm that was neither Nephoretti nor giant but something older. Sera came and sang with it, her voice weaving through the deep tones, and the needle's pulse seemed to slow, as if the Keeper was listening to the passing of something that had watched the east for longer than anyone remembered.

They laid Stone‑Jaw in the outer complex, among the fallen columns of the old workshops, where the grove giants had first built their shelters. The crystal of its jaw was cut and shaped into a marker, and Vine placed it at the head of the grave, facing east.

"It will watch still," Vine said, and the other giants made a sound that might have been agreement, or might have been mourning.

---

The years passed.

The shapes did not return. The watchers kept their vigil on the eastern tower, but the nights were empty, the mound's glow steady, the terraces still. Vine's crystal chain hung in the outer complex, a record of something that had been and was no longer, and the younger giants who had not seen the shapes began to treat the chain as history rather than warning.

But the world continued to change.

The light grew thinner. The seasons grew harsher. The harvest that had been thin became scarce, and the fields that had fed the city began to shrink. The scouts who ranged into the highlands returned with reports of snow that did not melt, of game that had vanished, of a cold that settled into the bones and did not leave.

"Something is wrong with the sky," Kael said one evening, standing on the wall with Teryn. His voice was the voice of an old man now, worn thin by a century of listening to the pulse. "The sun is the same, but the light that reaches us—it's less. As if something is standing between us and it."

Teryn looked up. The sky was the same sky it had always been—grey in winter, blue in summer, the stars bright and cold at night. But there was a quality to the light now that she did not remember from her youth. A thinness. A distance.

"The mound still glows," she said.

"Always." Kael's eyes were fixed on the east. "The same rhythm. The same light. As if nothing has changed there at all."

Below them, the city was quiet, the fires low, the work-songs muted. The children who had run through the plaza a decade ago were adults now, their faces marked by the long dimming, their voices carrying a note that had not been there in the old days. The giants moved through the outer complex with a slowness that had not been there before, their crystal patterns dimmer, their songs softer.

"Something is coming," Teryn said. It was not a question.

Kael did not answer for a long time. When he spoke, his voice was barely a whisper.

"The pulse has held for a hundred years. It will hold longer. But the world outside the boundary is changing, and we do not know why. The light fades. The cold grows. The shapes have withdrawn into the deep. And somewhere in the east, the mound still pulses, waiting for something."

He turned to her, his eyes old and clear.

"We have been watching the east for so long we forgot to watch the sky."

That night, Teryn stood on the eastern tower alone, looking up at the stars. They were the same stars that had shone on the landing, that had guided the survivors through the first winter, that had watched Garden Grove grow from a refuge into a city. But they seemed farther now, their light dimmer, as if something was pulling them away.

The mound glowed at the edge of the marsh, its rhythm steady, its terraces empty. The shapes had not returned in three years. The watchers still watched, but they watched for something that no longer came.

And in the chamber beneath the needle, the Keeper stirred in its dreaming, listening to a sky that was changing, to a light that was fading, to a cold that was creeping toward the walls.

The shapes were gone. But something else was coming
