chapter 112 Eastern system

Week One – Departure

Vale walked into the water at dawn. The eastern edge of the highest terrace, where worked stone gave way to raw flood-carved bedrock. Behind him, the needle's light still pulsed—a steady thrum in his chest.  The smooth stone the child had given him sat warm in his palm.

No pack. No weapon. Just the weight of the water rising around his ankles, his knees, his thighs.

The seabed dropped away before he was ready. The shelf broke sooner than it should have, and within a dozen paces the water was at his chest. . He knew the smell of salt and reeds, the soft give of mud between his toes. But here, that familiar smell was gone. Something colder replaced it—metallic, almost like blood left too long on a blade.

A faint shimmer clung to the surface ahead. Not light, not mist. A distortion that did not move with the current.

He stopped and turned. First Shore was already a line of low light on the horizon. The needle's warmth was steady, watching. But the water here felt different. As if it remembered something the shore had forgotten.

---

Three days later, he found the colour shift.

The sun was high, but the light had changed—less gold, more grey-green. He waded through a narrow channel between two low islands, bare stone with no reeds, no trees. The shimmer from that first morning lingered at the edge of his sight, never closer, never farther.

The water was cold against his waist, but the stone beneath his feet was warm. He had seen no survivors, no giants. The silence was complete except for the soft lap of water against his ribs.

Then the stone changed colour.

Grey-brown bedrock became a pale, almost luminous green—not algae, but the rock itself. When he stepped on it, a faint vibration ran up his legs. Not unpleasant. Foreign. Warmth from below, cold from above. A contradiction.

He knelt and touched the stone. The vibration pulsed once, then faded. In his chest, the needle's warmth flickered—not a warning, but a question. He did not know what this was. But he noted it: the east was not empty. It was structured differently.

---

By the fifth day, the islands were distant smudges. The shimmer was constant now—a line of distortion just beneath the surface, always ahead, never reached. Vale swam when he had to, walked when the bottom rose. The needle's pulse in his chest was fainter, as if the east was pressing against it. He had begun to count the hours by that weakening beat.

The tide turned. It should have flowed west, back toward First Shore. Instead, it continued east, faster, pulling the water toward the horizon. The current carried the same faint vibration as the green stone—a low hum he felt in his teeth.

He stopped swimming. Let the current hold him for a moment. This was not natural. Something ahead was drawing the water. He could turn back.

He did not.

He angled toward a nearby island to rest, the distortion still waiting on the horizon. By the end of the first week, the ground no longer felt like home. The further east he moved, the less the world answered the way it should. The needle's pulse was a thread growing thinner behind him.

He was not afraid. He was learning.

Week Two – Pattern Recognition

A shallow shelf between two bare islands. Similar to the place where he had found the green stone. The water was waist-deep, the sky the same grey-green. The shimmer waited on the horizon, unmoving.

Vale waded east, the smooth stone still in his hand. The needle's pulse was fainter now—a thread, not a beat.

The green stone appeared again. The same pale, luminous colour, the same warmth beneath the cold water. He stepped on it deliberately. The vibration ran up his legs—and this time he counted. One… two… three… four. It faded. He stepped again. One… two… three… four. The same interval. The same duration.

He waited. The water lapped against his chest. Then he moved sideways, off the stone, and the vibration did not come.

He tested it twice more, crossing the same patch at different angles. The vibration only triggered when he moved east. West or north, the stone was silent. He knelt, touched the green surface, and felt the hum build then stop.

This was not random. It responded to direction. The needle's warmth hesitated—a flicker, not a word. The east was not just strange. It was organized.

---

A few days later, the water grew quiet—too quiet. No islands nearby. The surface was glassy, unnaturally still. The shimmer was closer now, still unreachable, but it caught the light at a different angle, more visible when he looked east.

Vale swam slowly, conserving strength. The needle's pulse was a distant echo in his chest, no longer a guide. He relied on the sun, though its light felt thin.

He splashed his hand against the water. No sound. He called out, a low hum. The sound did not echo, did not travel. It died at his lips. Then he turned his head slightly, and a distant drip of water reached his ear from the wrong direction—behind him, though the drip had come from ahead.

He stopped. Turned in a slow circle. The shimmer shifted with his angle—visible at certain orientations, invisible at others. The environment was filtering what he perceived.

He closed his eyes. Listened. The drip came again—from his left, then his right, then nowhere. When he opened his eyes, the shimmer had moved. Not closer—but aligned with the direction of the false sound.

He swam ten paces east, then stopped. The sound changed pitch. He swam west, and the shimmer dimmed.

The world was not just strange. It was shaping what he could sense. The needle's warmth came delayed—a beat slower than his own heart.

---

Deeper now. The seabed dropped away and the water turned dark. The current was stronger, pulling steadily east. The shimmer was a constant line on the horizon, no longer fading at any angle.

Vale swam directly into the current. It resisted him—not violently, but with a steady pressure that tired his arms. The needle's pulse was almost gone, a phantom rhythm he felt only when he stopped moving.

The current intensified only when he swam east. He tested it: he turned north, and the current slackened. He turned east again, and it pushed harder. The vibration from the green stone was now in the water itself—a low hum that rose and fell with the current's strength.

The shimmer aligned perfectly with the current's direction. For the first time, he saw it not as a distortion but as a marker—something that indicated the system's focus.

He stopped fighting. He let the current carry him for a few heartbeats, as he had on the fifth day—but now it was intentional. The vibration hummed through his chest, matching the fading pulse of the needle. The needle's warmth was a flicker, uncertain.

He turned and swam east again, not fighting but testing. The current held him, carried him, did not harm him. It was not a trap. It was a coordination.

Something was organizing these behaviors. Something was guiding the water, the stone, the sound.

By the end of the second week, Vale understood: the anomalies were not random. They were a system—one that reacted to movement, direction, and perception. He chose to continue because the system had not attacked him. It had only adjusted.

The shimmer remained ahead. The needle's pulse was a whisper. And the needle's warmth was slower to respond, as if the east was pressing against her too.

He was not afraid. He was reading.

Week Three – Presence Begins

The water was deeper now, chest-high when he stood. The seabed was uneven, patches of green stone appearing without pattern. The shimmer was constant on the horizon, but it flickered—a hesitation he had not seen before.

Vale continued east, testing as he had for days. He stepped on a patch of green stone, expecting the familiar vibration—one… two… three… four. Nothing. He stepped again. Still nothing. He stepped west, off the stone, and the vibration triggered there—a place that should have been silent.

The rule broke. The vibration no longer responded to direction. He stood still, and the hum built beneath his feet without his movement. Then it stopped. He took a step east—the current shifted before his foot landed, pushing against him as if anticipating.

He paused. This was not a random error. It felt like an adjustment—as if something had corrected the system after his first test.

He stopped testing for a moment. Stood in the cold water, the shimmer flickering on the horizon. The needle's warmth was slow to return, a beat delayed. This wasn't just environmental anymore. Something was inside the system.

---

A threshold. The water deepened abruptly, the seabed falling away into dark. The green stone was gone. The shimmer was now a line of distortion that pulsed slowly, like a breath.

Vale swam. The current was steady but not strong. He crossed the depth line—and the environment changed.

A mass of water shifted against the current. Not a wave. A pressure, wide and silent, passing through him from left to right. His body turned with it, no resistance. Then a second pressure, from behind, pushing him forward. The shimmer distorted violently, folding in on itself, then snapped back.

No shape. No form. Only effect.

He treaded water, waiting. The pressures did not repeat. But the water felt occupied—as if something large and unseen had moved through and was now still, watching.

For the first time, he changed his behavior without testing. He stopped swimming east directly. He angled north, toward a nearby island, and watched the shimmer. It did not follow. It waited.

He was not afraid—but he was aware. Something was here. The needle's warmth was a flicker, uncertain, as if she could not fully feel what he felt.

---

A small island, bare, no green stone. The water around it was calm, but the shimmer was now directly east, no longer on the horizon. Closer. Still unreachable, but closer.

Vale left the island, swimming east again. He expected the current to resist, the vibration to hum—but instead, the sound died. Complete silence. No lap of water, no breath. His own movements made no noise.

He swam ten paces. The current pushed him north—not hard, but persistently. He corrected east. The current pushed harder. He tried again—the shimmer shifted angle, now southwest, behind him. A clear signal: this way.

He stopped. The silence held. He turned north, and the sound returned—a single drip of water, distant but real. The current slackened. The shimmer settled back to the east, but now at an angle that suggested a path.

He tested against it once—swam east directly. The current became a wall, not violent but immovable. He could not force it.

He chose to follow the redirection. Not surrender—recognition. Something did not want him going that way. Not yet. Not directly.

He swam north, the shimmer now at his right shoulder, a guide instead of a marker. The silence faded, replaced by the normal lap of water. A final realization settled in his chest:

The system was not neutral. It responded as if something was inside it. And that something had preferences.

By the end of the third week, Vale no longer asked what was wrong. He asked what was here. The needle's pulse was almost gone—a phantom rhythm he felt only when he was still. The needle's warmth was distant, as if the east was pressing against her too.

He was not afraid. But he was no longer analyzing. He was aware.

The shimmer remained ahead, closer now. And something unseen moved in the water beside him.

Week Four – Threshold

The water was darker now, deeper, the seabed lost below. No islands. No green stone. Only the shimmer—constant, directly east, no longer flickering.

Vale swam slowly. He had stopped testing. The current, vibration, silence, and shimmer had stopped varying. They were now a single, unified pressure: east.

The current pushed steadily east. The vibration hummed in the water itself, no longer tied to stone. The silence was complete—no splash, no breath, no distant drip. The shimmer did not shift angle; it waited directly ahead, a fixed point.

He stopped moving. The current held him in place, neither pushing nor releasing. The vibration continued. The silence held.

He moved east again. The current matched him exactly. Not resisting. Coordinating.

He realized: the system was no longer reacting to him. It was focused. Everything pointed the same way. He was not exploring anymore. He was being directed. The needle's warmth was a faint ember, barely there.

---

The water became a channel—not visually, but functionally. To his left and right, the current weakened. When he tried to swim north or south, the vibration faded and the silence broke into random, directionless sounds. The only stable direction was east.

He tested the boundaries. North: the current dropped, but the shimmer became erratic, flickering away. South: the same. East: everything aligned. West: he turned back the way he came—the current resisted, pushing him forward, not letting him retreat easily.

He was not trapped, but he was constrained. The system no longer allowed free exploration. Paths that were not east felt wrong—not dangerous, but discouraged. The shimmer shifted to block his view when he looked away from east.

He stopped trying to deviate. The system settled.

He understood: he was no longer moving through the system. He was inside it. The space had rules, and the rules enforced a direction. He swam east, not because he chose, but because the system had made other choices meaningless. The needle's warmth flickered once, then steadied—a silent acknowledgment.

---

The channel narrowed further. The water was still, dark, the shimmer now a handspan wide directly ahead. No horizon. No sky. Only that point of light.

He stopped. The current stopped with him. The vibration ceased. The silence held, but it was different. Expectant.

He moved forward one stroke. Pressure built—not water, but a weight against his chest, as if something was leaning into him. He stopped. The pressure released. He moved again. It returned.

He treaded water, still. The pressure did not come. He hummed—a low note, not a song, just sound. The silence broke for a single heartbeat: a distant, low pulse that was not his own, not the needle's. Then silence returned.

He changed nothing else. The pressure did not come again. But the shimmer brightened, just slightly.

He knew now. The presence was not just in the system. It was aware of him. It reacted when he moved. It listened when he made sound. It did not attack, but it watched.

He did not speak. He did not test further. He simply stayed still for a long moment, letting the presence feel his stillness.

Then he moved east again, one stroke at a time. The pressure returned, but it did not stop him. It only accompanied him.

---

By the end of the fourth week, Vale had reached the edge of something. Not a place—a boundary. The system was no longer subtle. The presence was no longer indirect.

He did not know what it was. He had not seen it. But it knew he was here.

The needle's pulse was gone—only a memory in his chest. The needle's warmth was a distant hum, waiting.

He was not afraid. But he was no longer moving freely.

He was inside. And something was watching..

The Rescue – Threshold Break

Vale swam east.

The pressure was still there—a weight against his chest, a presence that matched his movements. The shimmer was a fixed point ahead, no longer flickering. The silence held. He had accepted this. The system was not neutral. Something was inside it. And it knew he was here. He did not fight it. He simply moved.

The water was darker now, the seabed lost. No landmarks, no green stone, no islands. Only the channel—narrowing, pressing, directing. He swam for what felt like hours. The needle's pulse was gone. Luminaire's warmth was a distant ember. He was alone in the pressure, moving because moving was the only thing the system allowed.

Then the pressure hesitated.

It was subtle at first—a misalignment in the current. The steady push east faltered for a single heartbeat, then returned. Vale stopped. Listened. The silence broke unevenly. A drip of water—not from ahead, but from his left. Then a splash from behind. Then nothing. The shimmer flickered. Not gone—unstable. It bent, folded, then snapped back.

He turned his head slowly. The pressure did not follow. For the first time since entering the channel, the system was not coordinating with him. Something was wrong.

He swam forward again, and the current misaligned again—this time pushing west, then east, then west, as if it could not decide. The vibration hummed in the water, but the pitch was wrong, the rhythm broken. The shimmer dimmed.

Then he saw it.

A shape in the water. Not an island, not a rock. Something else. Something that did not belong. It was ahead and to his left, where the current was weakest. The shimmer bent around it, as if the system could not fully reach that space. The water there was different—quieter, less coordinated. Like a hole in the pattern.

Vale swam toward it. The pressure did not stop him, but it did not help him either. The current slackened, then died. The vibration faded. The silence became normal—not the enforced silence of the system, but the natural quiet of open water.

He reached the shape. It was a body. Floating face-up, arms loose, robes tangled in the current. The head was elongated, the proportions familiar—the same general physiology he had seen in the Igigi who had fallen from the sky centuries ago. But not like the ones he knew. This one wore a robe, not the structured gear of Enara's people. His posture was relaxed, collapsed—not the rigid control of the survivors from First Shore. And the water around him was wrong. Looser. Less aligned. As if the system did not fully agree with his presence.

Vale reached out and touched the body's chest. Cold, but not frozen. A heartbeat—slow, faint, but there. Alive.

He did not hesitate. He hooked an arm under the Igigi's shoulders and began to pull. The weight was awkward—the body limp, the robes waterlogged. Vale kicked hard, angling toward the nearest shore. There was no current to help him now. The system had withdrawn. Behind him, the shimmer flickered once, then snapped back to its distant alignment. The pressure did not return. The silence became ordinary.

He was alone with the body and the water.

He swam. The shore was a dark line ahead, closer than he had expected. The channel had narrowed further, but the system was no longer guiding him. He was simply moving. The Igigi's head lolled. Vale adjusted his grip, pulled harder. His arms burned. His chest ached where the pressure had been. He did not stop.

The bottom rose. Mud, then stone, then wet sand. Vale dragged the body out of the water, up the slope, away from the tide. His legs gave out when he reached dry ground. He knelt in the sand, breathing hard, his hands still gripping the Igigi's robe. The body did not move. Vale turned him over—gently, carefully. The face was pale, the eyes closed. The elongated skull was bare, no crystal patterns, no markings. The robes were torn in places, stained with salt and something darker. He pressed two fingers to the neck. A pulse. Weak, but there. Not dead.

Vale sat back on his heels. The water was dark, the sky darker. The shimmer was gone—or hidden by the distance. The pressure was absent. The system had released him. He looked at the Igigi. At the water. At the shore where they had landed. He did not know what he had found. Only that it had broken the system long enough for him to reach it.

He pulled the body higher up the beach—past the high tide line, onto a patch of gravel and packed sand. He turned him onto his side, clearing the airway. Then he removed his own outer robe—a thick, woven thing, worn but warm—and draped it over the Igigi's body, tucking the edges under. The night was cold. Vale sat close, shoulder to shoulder, sharing what warmth his own body could give. He did not build a fire. He had no tools, no tricks. Only his presence and his endurance.

He watched the Igigi's breathing. Shallow, but steady. He checked the pulse again—still there, still weak. He adjusted the robe once, twice, making sure the cold did not creep in. He did not sleep. He stayed awake, listening to the slow rhythm of the stranger's breath, feeling the cold settle around them. The stars came out. The water lapped against the shore, ordinary now, no longer coordinated. The shimmer did not return.

Vale sat in the dark, two bodies on a shore, one barely alive, one keeping him that way. He did not speak. He did not ask questions. He simply stayed.

The sky paled slowly, grey light bleeding over the water. Vale had not moved. His legs were stiff, his back sore, but the Igigi's breathing had deepened. The pulse was stronger. He checked again. Turned the body slightly. Pulled the robe higher. The face was still pale, but there was colour in the lips now—a faint flush of life returning. Vale sat back, watching the horizon. The system was gone. The shimmer was absent. He was just a man on a shore with a stranger who should not have been here. He did not know what came next. But the stranger was alive. And that was enough for now.

The grey light found them still on the shore. Vale had not slept. His back was against a driftwood log, his legs stretched toward the water, his eyes fixed on the stranger's breathing. The robe was still tucked around the Igigi's body. The tide had risen and fallen, leaving a dark line in the sand a few paces below them. The sky paled. A few clouds caught the first light, turning the colour of old copper. The water was calm, ordinary. No shimmer. No pressure. Just the slow lap of waves.

Then the stranger moved. A twitch of the fingers, a shift of the head. Vale leaned forward, watching. The Igigi's eyelids fluttered but did not open. His mouth opened slightly, closed. His breathing deepened, caught, then steadied. Vale did not speak. He simply waited. The sun cleared the horizon. The light turned gold, then white. The stranger's breathing found a rhythm—slow, but even. He was waking.

The sun was high when the Igigi opened his eyes. Vale saw it happen: a slow parting of the lids, the pupils contracting against the light. The eyes were dark—deep, almost black—and for a long moment they focused on nothing. Then they found Vale. The Igigi did not speak. He did not try to move. He simply looked, his breathing shallow but steady.

Vale held his gaze. "You are alive," he said. Quiet, not loud. "You are on a shore. I pulled you from the water."

The Igigi blinked. His lips moved, but no sound came out. He tried again—a rasp, a whisper, nothing shaped. Vale reached for a leafbowl he had found among the driftwood—. He lifted the Igigi's head gently and put the spout to his lips. A sip. A cough. Another sip. The Igigi swallowed. His eyes stayed on Vale.

"Can you walk?" Vale asked. The Igigi tried to move his legs. A wince. A shake of the head. Not yet. "Can you eat?" A slow blink. Maybe yes, maybe no. "Do you know where you are?" The Igigi's eyes drifted to the water, then back to Vale. He shook his head once, a small motion.

Vale nodded. "Neither do I. But there is a city to the west. First Shore. I was walking east when I found you." The Igigi said nothing. He closed his eyes, breathing hard, as if the few words had cost him. Vale sat back. "Rest. We will try to move when you are ready."

The sun hung low over the water, painting the shore in shades of orange and purple. The Igigi had slept again, fitfully, waking once to drink more water. His colour was better—less grey, more flesh. His breathing was deeper. Vale had found a few shellfish in the tide pools, cracked them open, offered the meat. The Igigi ate a little, chewed slowly, swallowed. He did not speak, but his eyes were clearer now. He watched Vale with something like curiosity.

When the light began to fade, Vale made a decision. "We stay one more night," he said. "You need the rest. Tomorrow we start walking west." The Igigi nodded—a small dip of the head. Vale pulled the robe higher over the Igigi's shoulders, then sat beside him, back against the log. The cold crept in, but they had each other's warmth. The stars came out. The water was dark. No shimmer. No pressure. Just the two of them on the shore. Vale did not sleep. He watched the stranger breathe, and waited for the morning.

The Return

Morning light, grey and cold. The tide had turned again, the water pulling back from the high line, leaving the sand wet and dark. Vale stood first. His legs were stiff, his back sore, but he did not show it. He looked at the Igigi—still sitting, still wrapped in the robe, his eyes open but unfocused.

"Can you stand?"

The Igigi nodded. He pushed up slowly, using the driftwood log for support. His legs held. He swayed once, then steadied. His breathing was shallow but even. Vale picked up his leafbowl, slung it over his shoulder. He did not offer help. He simply waited. The Igigi took a step. Then another. His feet were bare—his sandals had been lost in the water. The cold sand pressed between his toes.

"West," Vale said. Not a question. The Igigi nodded again. They left the shoreline.

Vale led. He moved across the rock like it was nothing—stepping from one dark slab to the next, his weight shifting automatically, his feet finding the flat spots without thought. The lava rock was rough, porous, sharp in places, but he had walked this kind of ground for centuries. It was as familiar as breathing. His eyes were not on the path. He glanced east. The horizon was empty. No shimmer. No pressure. No sign of the system that had guided him here. Just water and sky and the low line of distant islands. He glanced again. Nothing. He kept walking. His mind was not on the rock. It was on the absence.

Behind him, the Igigi moved differently. His steps were heavier. He placed each foot with care, testing the ground before committing his weight. The lava rock was not like the shores he knew—not the smooth sand of his homeland, not the worn stone of the deep channels. It was sharp, uneven, unpredictable. A ridge cut into his heel. He winced, shifted, kept moving. He did not complain. He simply adapted—slowly, deliberately, his breathing a little faster than it should have been. Vale did not look back. But he heard the difference. The heavier footfalls. The hesitation. He did not slow.

They reached a stretch where the rock was broken—a field of jagged shards, black and glassy, left by an old flow. Vale stopped. The Igigi stopped behind him. "This will cut you," Vale said. He pointed to a narrow path along the edge, where the shards gave way to packed ash. "Go that way." The Igigi looked at the path, then at his bare feet. He nodded. He stepped onto the ash. It was softer, easier. His pace picked up. Vale waited until the Igigi was past the worst of it, then followed. He did not say anything else.

The sun climbed higher. The rock grew warm beneath their feet. Vale had stopped glancing east. His attention had shifted—not to the path, but to the man behind him. The Igigi was still slow, still careful, but he was learning. He had begun to anticipate the rough patches, to shift his weight before the rock cut. His breathing was steadier. His eyes were on the terrain, not on his feet. He was adapting. Vale noticed. Not consciously—just a recognition, deep in his chest, that the stranger was not helpless. He was recovering. The Igigi noticed something too. Vale moved without thought, without hesitation, his body a machine of efficiency. But his head turned east every few minutes, even when there was nothing to see. He was looking for something that was not there. The Igigi did not ask. He simply watched.

They stopped on a low ridge, where the rock flattened and a few stunted bushes offered shade. Vale sat on a block of basalt, his legs stretched out, his eyes on the horizon. The Igigi sat a few paces away, his back against a boulder, his feet raw but not bleeding. Vale passed him the  leafbowl. The Igigi drank. Passed it back. They sat in silence. The wind was light, carrying the smell of salt and dry stone. A bird circled far above, too distant to name. Vale did not speak. The Igigi did not ask.

After a long while, the Igigi spoke. His voice was still rough, still quiet. "You were walking east when you found me." Vale nodded. "Why?" Vale considered the question. He could have said to see what grows. He could have said I do not know. He said neither. "Something was out there," he said. "I wanted to understand it." The Igigi looked at the eastern horizon. It was empty. "And now?" Vale stood. "Now I am walking west." He offered a hand. The Igigi took it, pulled himself up. His legs were steadier now. They began to move again.

The sun was low when they stopped for good—a shallow depression in the rock, sheltered from the wind, with a view of the water. They had made progress. Not far—perhaps a few miles—but enough. The rhythm was established. Vale moved automatically: checking the ground, finding a place to sit, setting the leafbowl between them. The Igigi sat where he was told, his feet aching, his body tired but stable. They understood each other slightly now. Not friends. Not allies. Just two people who had walked the same day and would walk again tomorrow. Vale looked east one last time. The horizon was dark. No shimmer. No pressure. Just the end of the day. He turned away.

---

The next morning, they moved again. The sun was high when the Igigi stopped. Vale continued a few paces, then turned. The Igigi was crouched beside a tide pool, his hand reaching for a strand of dark seaweed that clung to the rock. He pulled it free, examined it, then coiled it and tucked it into a loop on his belt. Vale said nothing. He waited. The Igigi stood, tested his footing, and walked on. His pace was better today—still careful, but less hesitant. The volcanic rock no longer surprised him. A few hundred paces later, he stopped again. This time for a patch of dried fiber—something like palm husk, bleached by the sun. He worked it loose, added it to the coil at his belt, and continued. Vale watched. He did not ask. He simply adjusted his own pace to match the Igigi's stops. By midday, the Igigi had gathered a small bundle: seaweed, fibers, a few strips of something that looked like bark. He tied it all together with a length of vine and slung it over his shoulder. Vale led on. The Igigi followed. The silence between them was ordinary now.

The terrain shifted. The lava rock gave way to a stretch of black sand, coarse and loose. The Igigi's bare feet sank slightly with each step. He did not complain. He simply walked. Vale glanced back once. The Igigi's eyes were on the ground, but his hands were busy—working the fibers as he walked, twisting them, testing their strength. He was not just collecting. He was preparing. Vale turned forward. Said nothing. The afternoon light turned gold. The wind picked up, carrying the smell of salt and dry stone. The Igigi added more material to his bundle—a piece of driftwood, thin and flexible; a scrap of cloth from his own torn robe. Vale noticed. Still said nothing.

They stopped at a sheltered hollow, a depression in the rock where the wind could not reach. The sun was low, the sky turning orange. Vale gathered driftwood.  He stacked the wood, found a flat stone, and struck it against another until a spark caught the kindling. The fire grew. Small, steady, ordinary. The Igigi sat near it, his back against a boulder, his bundle of materials beside him. He did not speak. He simply watched the flames for a long moment, then reached for the seaweed.

He worked slowly, deliberately. The seaweed was wet, but he twisted it, squeezed it, shaped it into cords. The fibers he wove between the cords, creating a mesh—flexible but strong. The bark strips became soles, layered and pressed flat. His hands moved with a quiet precision, practiced but unhurried. Vale watched from across the fire. He did not offer help. He simply sat, his back against the rock, his eyes on the Igigi's hands. The fire crackled. The wind picked up, then died.

Then the Igigi began to hum.

It was low—barely audible over the fire. A single note, held steady, then shaped into a pattern. It was not a song, not quite. It was a tone—a vibration that seemed to come from his chest, not his throat. The air around him shimmered faintly—not the shimmer of the east, but something softer, something that belonged to him. Vale leaned forward. The tone was familiar—not the needle's pulse, not Luminaire's warmth, but something that resonated in his chest nonetheless. He had heard something like it before, long ago, in the songs of the Nephoretti. But this was different. This was not a song of stone or vrill. It was something older.

The tone faded. The Igigi's hands had not stopped. The shoes were almost finished—a pair of sandals, woven from seaweed and fiber, with soles of layered bark. Vale waited until the silence settled. "What song was that?" he asked.

The Igigi looked up. His eyes caught the firelight, dark but warm. He did not smile, but something in his face softened. "That ain't a song, my boy." He tied the last knot, tested the sandals, and set them aside. "That's just how we talk."
