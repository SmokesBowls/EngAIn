Day One — The Second Morning

The light was still wrong when Sera woke.

She had slept in her clothes, wrapped in the woven blanket she had used since the first winter, and for a moment after opening her eyes she thought it was still night. The window of her dwelling faced east, toward the mound, and the sky beyond it was the color of water that had been standing too long.

She rose and walked to the needle.

The plaza was emptier than usual. The first shift of crafters usually passed through at this hour, their tools clinking, their work‑song rising to greet the dawn. Today she saw only a few figures, moving quickly, their voices muted. The cold that had come with the evening had not lifted; it clung to the stone, pressed against her face, made her breath mist in the thin light.

Vine was already at the needle's base. The young giant knelt with its hands pressed to the stone, its eyes closed, its crystal patterns dim. It did not look up when she approached.

Sera knelt beside it and began to sing.

The pulse answered immediately—steady, strong, the same rhythm that had held for a hundred years. But the song felt different today. The crystal veins caught her voice and carried it, but the response that came back was measured, deliberate. The Keeper was not reaching out to meet her. It was waiting for her to come to it.

She sang until her voice steadied, until the cold in her chest began to ease, until the light in the east shifted from grey to something slightly less grey. Vine joined her for the last verse, its voice a low rumble that vibrated through the stone, and for a moment the crystal veins brightened, pulsing with something that might have been warmth.

When she stopped, the light was no stronger than it had been when she started.

"The sun is up," she said. It was not a question.

Vine opened its eyes. "It has been up for an hour. The light does not reach us."

Sera looked at the sky. She could see where the sun should be—a brighter patch of grey, a place where the clouds seemed thinner. But the light that fell from it was pale, diffuse, as if it was traveling through something that scattered it before it could touch the ground.

"Will it get better?"

Vine was silent for a long moment. "I do not know."

Day Two — The Fields

The work had shifted.

Teryn walked the eastern fields at midday, though it felt like morning. The light was no stronger than it had been yesterday, and the shadows that stretched across the furrows were long and heavy, moving slowly as the unseen sun crept across the sky.

The harvest crews had started earlier than usual, working by the light of crystals charged with stored vrill. They moved through the rows with practiced efficiency, their hands finding the ripe grain, their voices low. The work‑songs that usually filled the fields were absent; no one had suggested it, but somehow the sound of singing felt wrong in this light.

Vairis met her at the edge of the western terrace, her ledger open, her face drawn.

"We're bringing in about two‑thirds of the usual yield," she said. "The plants are still growing, but slower. The light—" She gestured at the sky. "It's not enough."

"We have stores," Teryn said.

"For now. If this continues into the planting season..." Vairis shook her head. "We may need to shift to the western fields. They're getting more light, higher elevation."

Teryn looked west. The hills were visible through the thin light, their slopes darker than she remembered, the trees that covered them reduced to silhouettes against a sky that had no color.

"We wait," she said. "It's only been two days. It may correct."

Vairis gave her a look that was not quite agreement, but she closed her ledger and walked back toward the city without arguing.

Teryn stood in the field for a long time, watching the harvest crews work by crystal light, watching the shadows that should not have been so long at midday, watching the sky that refused to brighten.

It was not going to correct. She knew it the way she knew the pulse of the needle, the way she knew the weight of stone in her hands. But she was not ready to say it aloud.

Day Three — The Giants

The grove giants had changed.

Vine stood at the eastern wall now, not at the needle, and it stood for hours without moving. Its crystal patterns were brighter than they had been at dawn, pulsing in rhythm with the needle, but its eyes were fixed on the horizon, on the place where the mound should have been visible.

The other giants moved through the outer complex with a new purpose. They worked the stone of the old workshops, reinforcing walls that had stood for a century, clearing spaces that had been used for storage, making room for something Sera could not name. They did not sing as they worked. They moved in silence, their voices saved for something else.

Sera found Vine at midday, when the light was at its strongest—or what passed for strongest now. The sun was a pale disc behind the clouds, visible for the first time in three days, and its light fell on the marsh like water through gauze.

"You're watching the mound," she said.

"I am watching the light." Vine's voice was low, the words shaped carefully. "The swamp giants move when the light changes. They have always moved when the light changes."

"Are they moving now?"

Vine was silent for a moment. "They are waiting. They have been waiting for three days."

Sera followed its gaze. The mound was a dark shape against the lighter grey of the sky, its crystal veins barely visible in the dim light. She could not see the swamp giants—they were too far, or too still—but she knew they were there. She could feel them, the way she could feel the pulse of the needle, the way she could feel the weight of the darkness that pressed against the boundary.

"The Keeper is holding," she said. "The boundary hasn't weakened."

"The Keeper is holding," Vine agreed. "But the Keeper is not the only thing that knows how to wait."

Day Four — The New Normal

The rhythm of Garden Grove had changed.

Teryn walked the walls at what should have been dawn, though the light was no different from the light that had fallen at midday yesterday. The work shifts had been adjusted—the harvest crews started at the first sign of light, worked through the brightest hours, finished when the shadows began to stretch toward the walls. The crafters worked indoors now, using crystal light to shape stone and metal, and the work‑songs that had filled the plaza for a hundred years had been replaced by the low murmur of voices and the clink of tools.

The children ran through the streets as they always had, but their games were quieter, their voices lower, as if they had learned from the adults that something had changed. The market still operated, but the stalls closed earlier, and the food that was sold was measured more carefully.

Kael found her at the eastern tower, where Stone‑Jaw had stood its last watch. He moved slowly now, his century of years visible in his step, but his eyes were clear, and his hand was steady on the crystal shard at his belt.

"The pulse is steady," he said. "Stronger than it was at the start of the week. The Keeper is holding the boundary, maybe reinforcing it."

"Reinforcing it against what?"

He looked at the sky. "Against whatever is pressing against it."

She had felt it too—the weight in the air, the pressure that built as the light failed, the sense that something was pushing against the boundary, testing it, waiting for it to weaken.

"The scouts haven't seen anything," she said. "The marsh is quiet. The swamp giants haven't moved from the mound."

"The swamp giants don't need to move. Whatever is happening, they knew it was coming. They've been waiting for it."

Teryn looked at the mound, a dark shape against the grey sky. Its crystal veins pulsed with their slow, heavy rhythm, unchanged since the first day the light had thinned.

"They knew," she said. "And they prepared."

Kael nodded slowly. "So must we."

Below them, the city moved through its adjusted rhythms. The fires that had been lit at dawn were still burning, their light a pale orange against the grey. The grove giants moved through the outer complex, their forms dark against the stone, their voices silent. And somewhere in the east, the mound pulsed with a rhythm that was not the needle's rhythm, waiting for something that was almost here.

"This is not temporary," Teryn said. It was not a question.

Kael did not answer. He did not need to.

Morning — The Fourth Day

The light was the same.

Sera had stopped expecting it to change. She woke in the grey, walked to the needle in the grey, knelt in the grey. The pulse was steady, the Keeper waiting, the song passing between them without warmth or welcome. It was simply what she did now, as natural and as necessary as breathing.

Vine joined her for the morning song, as it had every day for three years, and they sang together in the dim light, their voices rising through the crystal veins, finding the Keeper where it waited in the stone. When they finished, the light was no different than it had been when they started.

"It's been four days," Sera said.

"It has."

"The sun is still there. The scouts saw it yesterday, above the clouds. But the light doesn't reach us."

Vine made a sound that might have been agreement, or might have been something older. "Something is between us and it. Something that scatters the light before it falls."

"Do you know what it is?"

The giant was silent for a long moment. When it spoke, its voice was lower than she had ever heard it.

"The old songs speak of times when the light failed. When the sky darkened and the cold came early and the world waited for something to pass. The swamp giants remember those times. They built the mound to watch for them."

"To watch for what?"

Vine turned its eyes to the east. "For what comes when the light fails."

Midday — The Stillness

The work had stopped.

Teryn felt it before she saw it—a silence that settled over the city like a held breath. She was on the eastern wall, walking the circuit she had walked every day for a hundred years, and suddenly the sounds that had become background to her existence were gone. The clink of tools from the crafters' quarter. The murmur of voices from the market. The calls of the children who ran through the streets.

She stopped and listened.

The marsh was silent. The reeds that usually rustled with the movement of small things were still. The channels that ran with water and life were quiet. The birds that had called from the trees for a century were gone.

Kael appeared beside her, his face pale, his hand pressed to the crystal shard at his belt.

"The pulse is steady," he said. "But something changed. A few minutes ago. The Keeper—" He stopped, his eyes widening.

"What?"

"It's not just holding. It's waiting. Like it's been waiting for something to happen."

Teryn looked at the sky. The sun was visible through the clouds, a pale disc that cast no shadow, and the light that fell from it was thin and cold. But there was something else now. A pressure in the air, a weight that pressed against her ears, her chest, her bones.

She looked east. The mound was dark against the grey sky, its crystal veins pulsing with a rhythm that seemed faster than it had been, heavier, more urgent. And on its slopes, the shapes that had been absent for three years were gathered. Dozens of them, maybe more, standing motionless, their faces turned to the sky.

"The giants know," Kael said. "They've been waiting for this."

Teryn turned to call down to the city, to send word to the grove giants, to prepare for something she could not name. But the words died in her throat.

The sky was moving.

The Break

It began as a flicker.

A point of light, high above the clouds, moving against the grey. Teryn saw it out of the corner of her eye and turned, her hand on the wall, her breath caught in her chest. It was not a star—it was too bright, too low, moving too fast. It was not the sun—it was wrong, its light flickering, shifting, breaking apart and reforming as it fell.

"What is that?" Kael's voice was sharp, the voice of a man who had not been young for a hundred years but who remembered what it was to fear.

She did not answer. She could not.

The light grew brighter as it fell, but it did not burn like fire. It was pale, cold, a light that seemed to pull the color from the sky as it passed. It flickered, stuttered, broke into three pieces and then reformed, and each time it changed, the pressure in the air grew heavier, the silence deeper.

Below her, the city was waking to the thing in the sky. Voices rose, sharp with fear, and she heard the clatter of tools dropped, the slam of doors, the high cry of a child. But the sounds were muffled, distant, as if the air itself was swallowing them.

She looked at the mound. The shapes on its slopes had not moved. They stood with their faces turned to the sky, their bodies still, and in their stillness she saw something she had not expected.

They were not afraid. They were watching. They had seen this before.

The light fell.

It was not a straight line—it curved, hesitated, broke apart again, and each fragment trailed light that did not fade, that hung in the air like a wound. The sound came a moment later, a deep rumble that shook the stone beneath her feet, that vibrated in her chest, that went on too long and ended too abruptly, leaving a silence that was worse than the sound had been.

The light struck the marsh.

It was miles away, beyond the mound, beyond the boundary, somewhere in the deep marsh where the swamp giants hunted and the water ran black. The impact threw up a wave of water and mud that rose higher than the walls of Garden Grove, that blotted out the mound, that hung in the air for a moment that stretched into forever.

Then it fell.

The wave crashed back into the marsh, sending a surge of water toward the boundary, toward the wall, toward the city. Teryn braced herself, her hands gripping the stone, but the water stopped at the line of light that marked the Keeper's boundary. It rose against it, foaming, churning, and then subsided, leaving the marsh darker, stiller, changed.

The silence that followed was absolute.

Teryn stood on the wall, her hands white on the stone, her breath coming in short gasps. Beside her, Kael was motionless, his eyes fixed on the place where the light had struck. Below her, the city was frozen, every face turned east, every voice still.

She looked at the mound. The shapes were gone. The terrace was empty, the crystal veins dark, and for the first time in a hundred years, the mound did not pulse.

She looked at the sky. The clouds were closing, the pale disc of the sun fading, the light that had been thin now barely visible. The thing that had fallen was gone, buried in the deep marsh, hidden by water and mud and the darkness that was already gathering.

She opened her mouth to speak, to say something, anything, but there were no words for what she had seen. There was only the silence, and the cold, and the weight of something new pressing against the boundary.

Kael's voice came from beside her, barely a whisper.

"What was that?"

She did not answer. She could not.

She stood on the wall and watched the marsh settle, watched the clouds close, watched the darkness that was not night gather around them. And in the silence that followed the impact, she heard something that made her blood run cold.

The mound was pulsing again. Not with its slow, steady rhythm, but with something faster, something that matched the beat of her heart, that pressed against the boundary, that waited.

The thing had fallen. The mound was awake. And the light did not return

Part 4— The Scar

The marsh had been torn open.

Renn stopped at the edge of a channel that should have been shallow enough to wade. It was gone—the water displaced, the mud thrown back, the reeds flattened for a hundred yards in every direction. A trench cut through the marsh, wide and deep, its edges still weeping water into the channels. And at the end of the trench, half‑buried in the mud and the broken reeds, something gleamed.

It was not stone. It was not crystal. It was something that caught the thin light and held it, that reflected it back in ways that hurt to look at. Metal, perhaps, or something that only looked like metal. Its surface was smooth in some places, crumpled in others, and where it had broken, the edges were sharp and dark.

"What is it?" Elara whispered.

Renn shook his head. He had seen the metal of the void needle, had touched the crystal veins that ran through it. This was not that. This was something else, something that had fallen from a sky that should have been empty.

"The cliff giants," Darian said, pointing.

They were on the far side of the trench, their massive forms silhouetted against the grey. There were five of them, the largest Renn had seen outside the mound. They stood at the edge of the impact zone, their bodies still, their faces turned toward the wreckage.

One of them looked up. Its eyes met Renn's across the trench, and for a moment neither moved.

Then the giant raised its hand. Not a threat—a signal. A warning.

"We should go," Theran said. "They don't want us here."

"The crash is in the marsh," Renn said. "It doesn't belong to them."

"It belongs to whatever fell out of the sky." Darian's voice was tight. "And they found it first."

The giant lowered its hand. The others turned, their bodies shifting, and Renn saw something in their posture that he had not expected. They were not guarding the wreckage. They were looking at something beyond it, something in the deep marsh where the water ran black and the channels twisted into maze.

"There's something else," Elara said. Her voice was strange, distant, as if she was seeing something the others could not. "In the water. Moving."

Renn followed her gaze. The marsh beyond the trench was still, the water dark, the reeds motionless. But there was a ripple where there should have been no ripple, a disturbance that spread from the wreckage toward the deeper channels.

The cliff giants saw it too. They turned as one, their bodies tensing, and Renn heard a sound he had never heard from them before—a low, sustained note that vibrated in his chest, that made the water shiver, that seemed to push against the darkness gathering in the marsh.

"Go," he said. "Now."

They ran.

Part 5 — The Split

The marsh swallowed them.

Renn led, his memory of the channels guiding them through the maze, his feet finding the paths that had been there for a hundred years. Behind him, he heard the others, their breathing sharp, their footsteps splashing in the shallow water.

The cliff giants did not follow. He glanced back once and saw them standing at the edge of the trench, their faces turned toward the wreckage, their song still rising into the grey sky. Whatever was moving in the water, they were not running from it. They were watching it.

The channels branched. Renn took the left fork, the one that led back toward the boundary, toward the safety of the Keeper's light. He heard Theran behind him, then Darian, their feet splashing in rhythm with his.

Then he heard Elara call out.

"I see something! Off the main channel—"

"Keep moving!" Renn shouted.

"There's something in the reeds—"

"Elara, come on!"

She took a step toward him. Then another. Her eyes were wide, fixed on something Renn could not see. Her mouth opened, as if to speak, and then—

The water moved.

It was not a ripple. It was something rising from the channel, something dark and quick, and before Renn could shout, before Elara could move, it was gone. And she was gone with it.

The channel was empty. The water was still. The reeds were silent.

"Elara!" Renn's voice cracked across the marsh, but there was no answer. Only the grey sky, the dark water, the silence of something that had been there and was no longer.

"She's gone. And if we stay, we'll be gone too."

Renn looked at the channel where Elara had stood. The water was dark, the reeds undisturbed, and there was no sign that anyone had ever been there. But the ripple was spreading from the right fork, moving toward them, slow and deliberate.

He ran.

Part 8— The Rescue

They came at what should have been dawn.

Renn led them through the channels, following the thread of pulse that Kael read from his crystal shard. It was faint, Elara's signal, but it was there, a thread of light in the darkness, pulling them east.

Vine's kin moved ahead, their massive forms silent in the water, their eyes seeing what Nephoretti eyes could not. The marsh was still, the reeds motionless, the channels running dark. The mound pulsed on the horizon, its rhythm slow, deliberate, and somewhere in the deep marsh, the thing that had fallen waited in the dark.

They found the cave at the edge of the deep channels, its entrance half‑hidden by fallen stone, its darkness absolute. The swamp giants had been there—Renn could smell them, could see the marks of their passage in the mud—but they were gone now, withdrawn to the mound, waiting for something.

He motioned to the others, and they moved forward, their boots silent on the stone, their hands on their weapons.

Inside, the cave opened into a chamber that glowed with faint light. Elara was there, bound but alive, her face streaked with mud and tears. And beside her, huddled against the wall, were three figures that made Renn's breath catch in his throat.

The figures did not move. They watched him with eyes that glowed faintly in the darkness, their bodies still, their hands open.

The Igigi worked beside her now, not singing but measuring, their long fingers tracing the crystal veins, their minds calculating the slow healing of the spire's fracture. The crack had shrunk—not much, but enough. In another thousand years, Enki said, it might be sealed. In two thousand, the needle might be whole.

None of them would live to see it. But the work continued.

The Darkness deepened. The light that had been thin grew thinner, the grey sky pressing down on the marsh like a lid. The cold that had come early stayed late, and the growing seasons that had been short grew shorter. The western terraces, Nin's great project, fed the city through the worst years, but the yield was never enough. They learned to eat less. To store more. To stretch what they had.

Children were born in the grey, grew up in the grey, and some of them grew old in the grey without ever seeing the sun. They learned the stories of the golden light, the copper marshes, the warmth that had once come from the sky. But they did not believe. Not really. The grey was all they knew.

Part 19— The Watchers

The mound did not change.

Vine had grown old as well, its crystal patterns dimming, its steps slow. It still stood watch on the eastern wall, but younger giants took the night shifts now, their eyes sharp, their bodies still. The mound pulsed with its slow, heavy rhythm, unchanged for centuries. The shapes that had moved on its slopes had not returned. The swamp giants had withdrawn into the deep marsh, their patrols rare, their presence felt only as a pressure at the edge of the boundary.

"The mound is waiting," Vine said to Kael one evening, as they stood together on the wall. Kael was ancient now, his body bent, his hands gnarled, but his eyes were still clear.

"Waiting for what?"

Vine was silent for a long moment. "For the light to return. Or for it to fail completely. I cannot tell which."

Kael looked at the mound, at the pulse that had not faltered in three hundred years. "The Igigi say the Annunaki will come back. When the light returns."

"The Igigi say many things." Vine's voice was soft. "They have been here for three hundred years, and I still do not know if they are saving us or measuring us for something else."

Kael nodded slowly. "Neither do I. But the needle is healing. The Keeper is waking. And the light—" He looked at the sky, at the grey that had not lifted in centuries. "The light will return. It has to."

Part 20— The First Hint

It happened in the four hundredth year of the Darkness.

Sera was gone by then—she had passed into the stone a century ago, her voice stilled, her songs carried on by her students. Enki had grown old, his long fingers still counting, his eyes still calculating, but his body thin and tired. Nin had died in a collapse while mapping the deep channels, and Damu had withdrawn into the workshops, speaking only to those who needed her calculations.

The city had changed. The walls had been rebuilt three times. The outer complex had grown into a second city, its streets wide enough for giants, its buildings shaped by generations of Igigi mathematics. The Nephoretti who lived there had never known the sun. They had never known the world before the grey.

The first hint came at dawn.

Teryn was long dead—her name was carved into the stone of the eastern tower, beside Stone‑Jaw's crystal. The council was led by a woman named Mora, who had been born in the Darkness and had never expected to see anything else. She stood on the wall with Vine's successor, a young giant named Thorn, and watched the eastern sky.

The grey was thinner.

It was not bright. It was not warm. But there was a quality to the light that had not been there in four hundred years—a texture, a depth, as if the clouds were not as thick as they had been.

Mora's hand went to the crystal at her throat. "Is it...?"

Thorn's eyes were fixed on the horizon. "I do not know."

They stood together, Nephoretti and giant, and watched the light grow. It was slow, so slow that at first they thought they were imagining it. But the grey began to shift, to pale, and for the first time in four hundred years, they saw something that was not cloud.

A shadow. A shape. The outline of something behind the grey.

"The sun," Mora whispered. "It's still there."

Thorn made a sound that might have been a word, or might have been a prayer. "It never left. We just could not see it."

Part 21 — The Return

The light did not return in a day, or a year, or a decade.

It came slowly, a gradual thinning of the grey, a gradual warming of the cold. The clouds that had covered the sky for four centuries began to break, not all at once, but in patches, in streaks, in moments that lasted an hour and then closed again. The first time actual sunlight touched the marsh, the people of Garden Grove stood in the streets and wept.

The children who had never seen the sun did not understand. They pointed at the light, asked what it was, tried to catch it in their hands. The elders—those few who remained from before the Darkness—sat in the warmth and closed their eyes and remembered.

Enki stood at the needle's base, his long fingers pressed to the stone, and felt the pulse shift. It was not weaker or stronger. It was different. The Keeper, whatever it was, wherever it was, had felt the light return.

"The Darkness is ending," he said to Damu, who had come out of her workshop for the first time in years. "But the Annunaki will come. They always come when the light returns."

Damu looked at the sky, at the pale sun that was beginning to burn through the clouds.

"Then we will be ready," she said. "Or we will not. Either way, the tower is healing. And the Keeper is waking."

Enki nodded slowly. He looked at the eastern wall, at the mound that still pulsed in the distance, unchanged, patient, waiting.

"The Keeper is waking," he said. "But I do not know what it will find when it opens its eyes."

---

The light grew stronger. The cold retreated. The marsh, which had been grey for four centuries, began to show color again—the deep green of sedge, the copper of channels, the gold of reeds. The fields that had been thin began to thicken. The channels that had been dark began to run clear.

Garden Grove had survived the Darkness. And now, as the sun rose on a world that had not seen it in four hundred years, the city prepared for whatever came next.

The long night was over. But the dawn brought its own shadows.

Part 22: Let There Be Light

— The Breaking

It happened in the spring.

Mora stood on the eastern wall at dawn, as she had done every morning for thirty years. The grey sky pressed down on the marsh, the same grey that had pressed down on her mother, and her mother's mother, and all the generations born since the Darkness came. She had stopped expecting it to change. The grey was the world. The grey was all there was.

Then the grey cracked.

It was not a sound—it was a light, thin as a thread, cutting through the clouds from a place she had never seen. Mora raised her hand to shield her eyes, and for a moment she did not understand what she was seeing. The thread widened, spread, and the grey began to peel back like skin from a wound.

The sun.

She had heard the stories. The elders spoke of it in the winter gatherings, when the cold pressed against the walls and the fires burned low. They said it had been gold, warm, a light that painted the marsh in colors she could not imagine. She had never believed them. Not really.

Now she stood on the wall and watched the stories come true.

The light fell on the marsh, and the marsh woke. The reeds that had been grey for four centuries turned green. The channels that had been dark ran copper and gold. The stone of the walls, which had known only shadow, began to glow with a warmth she could feel on her skin.

Below her, the city was waking. Voices rose—not in fear, but in wonder. Children who had never seen the sun pointed at the sky and asked questions no one could answer. Elders who had been born in the Darkness sat on the ground and wept.

Thorn stood beside her, the young giant's crystal patterns bright in the new light, its eyes fixed on the horizon.

"It is warm," Thorn said. Its voice was soft, almost reverent. "I did not know the light could be warm."

Mora looked at the mound. It still pulsed in the distance, its rhythm unchanged, its crystal veins catching the sunlight and throwing it back in patterns that seemed almost beautiful. Almost.

"The sun is back," she said. "But the mound is still there."

Thorn made a sound that might have been agreement, or might have been something else. "The mound has always been there. It will always be there. The question is what it will do now that the light has returned."

Part 23— The Celebration

The city did not work that day.

The fields stood empty. The workshops were silent. The walls, which had been guarded for four centuries without pause, were abandoned to the children who ran along their tops, chasing the light.

Mora had not ordered it. No one had. The people simply stopped, and the city held its breath, and for the first time in four hundred years, the work‑songs were replaced by something else. Laughter. Tears. The sound of a world remembering what it had lost.

Enki sat at the base of the needle, his long fingers pressed to the stone, his eyes closed. The light fell on his face, and for a moment he looked almost young—the scars on his wrists still raw, the years still heavy on his shoulders, but his face soft in a way she had never seen.

"The tower feels it," he said when Mora approached. "The light. The warmth. It has been waiting."

"For the sun?"

"For something to change." He opened his eyes. They were old, older than any eyes she had seen, but they were clear. "The Annunaki will feel it too. They will know the Darkness has lifted. They will come."

"Not today."

"No." He looked at the sky, at the sun that was climbing toward its zenith. "Not today. But soon. The tower is still broken. The Keeper is still absent. We are not ready."

Mora knelt beside him. "Then we become ready."

Part 24— The First Touch

Damu came out of her workshop.

She had not stood in the sunlight since before the Darkness fell. Her skin, the color of deep water, seemed almost translucent in the new light, and she blinked against the brightness like a creature emerging from a cave.

She walked to the needle, her steps slow, her long fingers tracing the air. Enki rose to meet her, and the two Igigi stood together at the base of the spire, their heads tilted, their hands raised.

"The crack has healed," Damu said. "Not fully. But enough."

"Enough for what?" Mora asked.

Damu looked at her, and for a moment her face was unreadable. "Enough to begin. The tower needs to be whole before the Annunaki return. Not perfect—just whole. The Keeper needs a vessel that can hold its weight."

She turned to Enki. They spoke in their own language, the words too fast for Mora to follow, the patterns of their speech like mathematics given sound. When they turned back, their faces were set.

"We will need materials," Enki said. "Crystal from the highlands. Stone from the deep marsh. Tools we do not have. It will take time."

"How much time?"

Enki looked at the needle, at the light pulsing in its core. "A thousand years. Perhaps more. But we will begin today."

He placed his hands on the stone. Damu placed hers beside his. And for the first time in four hundred years, the Igigi touched the needle with purpose.

The pulse shifted.

It did not weaken or strengthen. It changed—deepening, slowing, as if the Keeper was turning in its sleep, aware that something was different. The crystal veins glowed brighter, and the light that fell from them was not the thin grey of the Darkness, but something warmer, something that matched the sun.

Mora watched them work, the two Igigi who had fallen from the sky, who had been thrown away by their masters, who had stayed in a world that was not their own. They were thin, scarred, old. But their hands were steady, and their minds were sharp, and they did not stop.

"The tower will be whole," Enki said, more to himself than to her. "The Keeper will wake. And when the Annunaki come, they will find a world that is not as weak as they left it."

Part 25— The First Night

The sun set in colors Mora had never seen.

The sky, which had been grey for four centuries, turned gold and copper and deep purple. The marsh caught the light and threw it back, the channels running with fire, the reeds burning with a light that was not flame. The people of Garden Grove stood on the walls and watched the sun die, and they did not fear the darkness that followed because they knew—for the first time in their lives—that the light would return.

Mora stood on the eastern wall with Thorn, watching the stars appear. They were brighter than she had imagined, colder, more numerous. The mound pulsed in the distance, its rhythm unchanged, its light faint against the new brightness of the sky.

"Tomorrow," Thorn said, "the work begins."

"Yes." Mora looked at the needle, at the light pulsing in its core, at the two Igigi who still knelt at its base, their hands on the stone. "Tomorrow, we rebuild."

"And the Annunaki?"

Mora looked at the sky, at the stars that had been hidden for four centuries, at the darkness between them that seemed deeper than she remembered.

"They will come. But not tomorrow. And when they do, we will be ready."

The sun set. The stars rose. And in the shadow of the needle, the Igigi worked through the night, their long fingers counting, measuring, healing.

The Darkness was over. The long work had begun.
