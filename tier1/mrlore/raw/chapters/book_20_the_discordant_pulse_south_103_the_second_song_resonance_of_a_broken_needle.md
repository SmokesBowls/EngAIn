

Chapter 103: The Second Song – Broken Ground

I. The Southern Landing

The Ethereal Realm, moments after the first thousand Nephoretti leaped through the Veil. The six Aeon Keepers converged in the space between spaces, their consciousnesses vibrating with residual effort.

Lyaris turned her awareness toward the southern hemisphere. The first thousand had landed cleanly—their descent guided by the steady pulse of the northern void needle, which had survived the shattering intact. But the south… she extended her perception through the vrill currents and felt it immediately: a wound in the dimensional fabric. The southern void needle, ancient and immense, had cracked during Tiamat’s destruction. Its pulse was erratic, sending shockwaves through the Veil that made the friction between realms jagged and unpredictable.

“The southern needle is compromised,” she said. “The turbulence will be… severe.”

Korath’s consciousness hardened. “Then we craft the second thousand for survival. Denser thought‑seeds. Redundant consciousness matrices. They must endure forces that would tear the first group apart.”

Theron pulsed agreement. “Probability streams show high fragmentation. Some seeds may not survive the transition. Others will scatter across hundreds of miles. The Veil in that region behaves like a storm—localized collapses, sudden density spikes.”

“And the Annunaki?” Vaelith asked. “If the needle is damaged, they cannot maintain a stable presence there. The southern continent may be empty.”

Mordain’s awareness brushed against ancient archives. “Not entirely. The void needle itself remains—a relic from before our time. The Annunaki may have attempted to study it once, but without a functional needle, they cannot hold a foothold. The southern continent has been untouched for millennia.”

A silence passed between them—the equivalent of a shared breath.

“Then we send the second thousand,” Lyaris said. “We give them resilience. And we give them something more: as an emergency protocol, the instinct to seek shelter. When they land in chaos, they will need to find stability. The damaged needle, even broken, radiates a rhythm they can anchor to.”

The crafting took longer this time. Each thought‑seed was woven with thicker vrill strands, their consciousness‑patterns compressed into matrices that could withstand the chaotic pressures of the southern Veil. Lyaris worked with painstaking precision, layering in not only the standard Nephoretti template—geomancy, vrill‑sense, language capacity—but a quiet, persistent pull toward the damaged needle’s pulse. A sense that order could be found even in ruin.

When the thousand seeds were ready, they released them.

The descent was nothing like the first. The northern seeds had glided through the Veil like stones through calm water. These were flung into a maelstrom. The damaged needle’s erratic pulses created vortices in the dimensional fabric, twisting the vrill currents into knots. Some thought‑seeds shredded before they could form bodies. Others tore apart mid‑transition, their consciousness fragments scattering into the quantum foam.

But more than half made it through.

Their manifesting bodies, forged denser and more resilient, crashed onto the southern continent in bursts of raw energy that lit the bruised sky like falling stars.

II. First Ground

On the ground, Teryn opened her eyes to darkness and the smell of sulfur.

She lay in a crater of shattered rock, her newly‑formed body aching with the violence of arrival. Around her, the air shimmered with residual vrill—wild, unsteady, nothing like the stable currents the Aeon Keepers had described. She pushed herself up on trembling arms and looked out.

The landscape was jagged, volcanic. Rivers of cooling basalt snaked between black cliffs. In the distance, a mountain vented steam into a bruised sky. And there, half‑buried in a collapsed hillside, she saw it: a spire of metal and crystal, towering higher than any structure from the memory‑seeds the Aeon Keepers had implanted.

The void needle. Ancient. Broken.

Its crystalline core was exposed to the elements, fractured along a diagonal fault that leaked faint pulses of light—each one sending a shudder through the Veil above. Even damaged, it radiated a deep, rhythmic hum that Teryn could feel in her bones. A heartbeat. A wound that still pulsed.

Around her, other Nephoretti were rising from their own craters. Some moved with purpose; others stumbled, disoriented. A few craters held only scattered vrill‑ash—those who had not survived.

Teryn counted quickly. In her immediate vicinity: perhaps two dozen survivors. How many more had landed beyond the ridge, she could not yet tell.

She took a step toward the broken needle, drawn by the quiet compulsion the Aeon Keepers had woven into her—not curiosity about the structure itself, but an instinct for stability. The pulse, even erratic, was the most ordered thing in this chaotic place.

As she approached, she noticed that the ground around the needle was different. Not volcanic ash, but worked stone—ancient paving, cracked and overgrown but unmistakably shaped. A plaza of some kind, built around the spire’s base. Whoever had erected this structure had intended it to be approached with reverence.

She reached the edge of the plaza and stopped. Up close, the needle’s scale was overwhelming. The fracture ran diagonally through its core, exposing layers of crystal and metal that had grown like sedimentary rock over eons. The pulse that emanated from it was slow, labored—a heart struggling to beat.

Behind her, other Nephoretti were gathering at the plaza’s edge. None spoke. The memory‑seeds they carried included language, but this moment required no words. They stood together, survivors of a violent birth, staring up at a monument from a time before memory.

Teryn felt the pulse of the broken needle resonate with something deep in her newly‑formed body. Not a call. Not a command. Simply… presence. A reminder that even in ruin, order could persist.

She turned to face the others. About two dozen survivors, by her count. Their faces were smudged with ash, their bodies still adjusting to the strange weight of physical form. But their eyes held the same recognition: they were not alone, and this place—broken as it was—would be their beginning.

“We build,” she said, the word coming out rough, her voice still learning itself. “We build, and we learn. The pulse of this place—it can guide us.”

No one argued. They were too exhausted, too raw, too new. But as one, they turned back toward the broken needle and began to move—not into it, but around it, spreading out to explore the ancient plaza, to find shelter among the fallen stones, to begin the work of survival.

High above, the Veil continued to churn, stirred by the needle’s erratic pulse. But here on the ground, for the first time since their violent arrival, there was stillness.

III. The First Attack

The first dawn on the southern continent came not as light but as a slow bleeding of darkness into the color of bruised fruit. Teryn watched it from the edge of the ancient plaza, her back against a fallen block of worked stone, her hands still raw from the work of survival.

Several days had passed since the leap. Several days since she had clawed her way out of a crater and found the others breathing the sulfur air. She was learning what it meant to have a body that could ache, could hunger, could shiver when the wind swept down from the volcanic highlands.

The others were scattered across the plaza now, each finding their rhythm. Some gathered the glassy stones that littered the edges of the ancient paving, stacking them into low windbreaks. Others explored the perimeter where the worked stone gave way to twisted swamp—knee-deep water tinted the color of rust, trees with roots that walked, air so thick with moisture it felt like breathing through wet cloth. A few had ventured toward the broken needle itself, standing in its shadow, heads tilted as if listening to the erratic pulse that vibrated through the ground.

Kael was one of them. He stood closest to the fracture, his newly-formed features creased in concentration. Of all the survivors, he seemed most attuned to the needle’s rhythm—or perhaps most troubled by it. When Teryn approached, he didn’t turn.

“It’s slowing,” he said. His voice still had the rough edge of newness, words shaped by a throat that had never spoken before. “The pulse. It’s not steady. It… falters.”

Teryn felt it too, now that he mentioned it. A hesitation in the deep thrum that had guided them since landing. Like a heart skipping beats.

“The Aeon Keepers said it was damaged,” she said. “We knew it wouldn’t be stable.”

Kael’s jaw tightened. “Damaged implies it was whole once. This feels like dying.”

They stood in silence, watching the fractured core leak pale light into the morning. Then a shout came from the eastern edge of the plaza—one of the sentries, her voice sharp with alarm.

Teryn moved before she thought, her body responding faster than her mind. She reached the eastern edge breathless, just in time to see what had alarmed the sentry.

Movement in the swamp. Not the slow sway of vegetation, but something deliberate. Something large.

The mist parted, and Teryn saw them.

Three figures, each taller than the fallen stones that dotted the plaza. Thirty feet at least, their bodies composed of living stone and crystallized minerals. Pelagor transformed by millennia of survival in Earth’s crucible. Giants.

But these were not the docile creatures The Keepers had observed on the northern continent. These moved with purpose. With aggression. Their massive hands were clenched into fists, their stony faces set in expressions that Teryn’s newly-formed instincts read as hostility.

One of them slammed its fist against a swamp tree, shattering the trunk to splinters. The sound cracked through the morning like thunder.

“Back,” Teryn said, her voice low but carrying. “Back to the needle.”

The other Nephoretti were already moving, abandoning their half-built shelters, gathering toward the center of the plaza where the broken spire loomed. But the giants were faster than their size suggested. The lead one—its hide mottled green-black with swamp growth, its limbs thicker and more jagged than the northern variants—lumbered onto the ancient paving in three strides.

It stopped at the edge of the plaza, its massive head swiveling as it took in the Nephoretti. Its eyes, deep-set and glowing faintly with internal heat, fixed on Teryn.

She stood her ground. Not bravery—she simply had nowhere to go that would matter. The others were behind her, clustered at the needle’s base. If this creature wanted to reach them, it would have to go through her.

“We mean no harm,” she said, though she had no idea if the giant could understand her.

The giant tilted its head, and for a moment Teryn thought she saw something in its eyes—not understanding, but recognition. Then it raised its fist.

She dove sideways as the blow came down, the stone beneath her shattering into shards that sliced her arm. She rolled, came up bleeding, and saw the other two giants advancing from the flanks. They moved with the same deliberate menace, their eyes fixed not on her but on the group clustered at the needle.

They were hunting.

“Inside!” Kael’s voice cut through the chaos. He had found a gap in the needle’s base—a fissure where the ancient structure met the ground, wide enough for a Nephoretti to squeeze through. The others were already pushing toward it.

Teryn ran, her injured arm clutched to her chest. She scrambled through the gap, feeling the rough crystal scrape her shoulders, and tumbled into darkness.

Inside the needle was not empty.

The space opened into a chamber that pulsed with the same erratic rhythm as the spire itself. Veins of crystal lined the walls, some dark and dead, others flickering with pale light. The floor was smooth, worked by hands older than memory. And the air—the air hummed.

Teryn counted heads in the dimness. Most of them had made it, but a few were missing—left outside, perhaps already dissolved into vrill-ash.

Outside, the giants circled the needle. Their footsteps shook the ground. One of them tried to reach into the fissure, its massive fingers scraping against the opening, but the gap was too narrow. It pulled back with a sound like grinding stones—frustration.

“They can’t get in,” someone whispered. “We’re safe.”

Kael shook his head. He was pressed against one of the crystal veins, his hand flat against its surface. “This place is dying,” he said quietly. “The pulse is weakening. When it stops…” He didn’t finish.

And then, from deeper within the needle, came a sound. A drip, slow and rhythmic, like water finding its way through stone. Or like something waking.

Teryn turned toward the darkness that led deeper into the needle’s heart, and felt the first stirring of a new kind of fear.

IV. The First Harmony

The sound that shifted the needle’s pulse came from Sera.

She hadn’t meant to make it. Huddled against the crystal wall, her hands pressed over her ears to block out the giants’ footsteps, she had begun humming—a low, wavering note that vibrated in her throat without conscious intent. A sound from a memory she shouldn’t have, woven into her like all the others.

The pulse changed.

It was subtle—a hesitation, then a shift in rhythm, the erratic beat smoothing into something almost steady. The crystal veins along the walls flickered, their pale light strengthening for a breath, then fading again. But the shift was enough. The giants’ footsteps above paused. A long silence. Then, slowly, the heavy tread receded.

Sera stopped humming. The pulse returned to its irregular, labored beat.

Kael’s hand was pressed flat against the crystal vein beside him. His eyes were wide. “It responded,” he said, his voice barely a whisper. “To you.”

No one had an explanation. But when Teryn led them back out into the fading light, the plaza was empty. The giants had withdrawn into the swamp.

They worked through the night. Fallen stones were dragged into rough walls, stacked between the broken paving and the needle’s base. Those with stronger geomantic sense learned to shape stone with their hands, pressing vrill into the cracks until the blocks fused. By dawn, they had a wall. Low, barely chest-high, but enough to slow an approach.

More survivors arrived that day. Some had landed close, hidden in the roots of the walking trees. Others had walked for days, following the needle’s pulse like a beacon.

Before long, nearly a hundred Nephoretti had gathered at the plaza. The low wall had grown into a proper barrier. And when Sera hummed again—testing, uncertain—the pulse steadied for a moment. The others noticed. Some began to hum along. The pulse responded, stronger this time, and the crystal veins along the needle’s exterior glowed faintly in the twilight.

“It likes sound,” Kael said, watching the light fade. “Or it needs it.”

V. The Safe Perimeter

In the days that followed, the plaza transformed.

What had been a scattering of fallen stones became something approaching a settlement. The wall grew from chest-high to a proper rampart, fused stone reinforced with vrill-charged crystal salvaged from the needle’s outer fractures. Shelters multiplied against the spire’s base—not the crude windbreaks of the first week, but proper structures with sloping roofs and interior spaces divided by woven reeds.

Teryn walked the perimeter each morning, her hand brushing the wall’s surface, feeling the faint vibration of the needle’s pulse through the stone. It was steadier now, not by much, but enough. And something else: the giants never came close.

“They stop at the old road,” Kael reported one evening, pointing to a line of moss-covered stones that marked the eastern boundary of the plaza’s original extent. “I’ve watched them for many days. They patrol the lowlands, they hunt along the river, but they don’t cross that line.”

Jaren had made similar observations to the north. “It’s like the needle’s pulse pushes them back.”

“Then why did they attack us at all?” someone asked.

Teryn considered. “Maybe the pulse was weaker then. Or maybe they were testing—seeing if the needle’s protection still held.”

The implication settled over them: the needle kept them safe, but only so long as its pulse endured. And its pulse was still erratic, still weakening, still dependent on forces they didn’t understand.

Sera hummed more often now. Not intentionally—it had become a habit, a background rhythm to her work. Others had picked it up, their voices rising in wordless song as they shaped stone or wove reeds or stood watch. The pulse responded each time, steadying, its light strengthening for a few heartbeats before fading again.

No one spoke of it as magic. It was simply something that happened when they worked together.

VI. The Outer Structures

Before long, they began to explore beyond the immediate plaza.

The ground surrounding the needle was not natural. Millennia of swamp growth had buried much of it, but beneath the muck and twisted roots, they found evidence of something larger—a complex that extended outward in concentric rings, its outer edges lost in the dense vegetation.

“This wasn’t just a tower,” Kael said, standing at the edge of what might have been a courtyard. Fallen columns lay half-submerged in black water, their surfaces carved with patterns that predated anything in the Aeon Keepers’ memory-seeds. “This was a city. Or the beginning of one.”

They cleared the nearest structures first: a ring of low buildings that might have been workshops or dwellings, their stone roofs collapsed but their walls still standing. Inside, they found nothing but sediment and the roots of swamp trees—but the walls themselves held traces of vrill, faint as old scars.

“The Keepers didn’t build this,” Jaren said, running his hand over a carving that seemed to shift under his fingers. “This is older. Much older.”

Teryn remembered the Aeon Keepers’ words: The void needles were here before us. This place had been ancient when Tiamat was young. And something had lived here, something that understood vrill in ways she was only beginning to sense.

The exploration yielded little else that season, but it gave them something they hadn’t had before: a sense of scale. The needle was not alone. It was the heart of something that had once spread across this land.

“We should send scouts further out,” Renn suggested. “If we’re safe here, others might be looking for us.”

Teryn agreed. “Groups of three. Stay within a day’s travel. Mark the safe paths. And if you find survivors—bring them back.”

VII. The Delta Group

Hundreds of miles west, where the continent’s spine fractured into a maze of tidal channels and mangrove forests, a different story was unfolding.

Venn had never been meant to lead. Her group had landed in the delta—several dozen through the Veil together, only about half survived the first hour. The rest scattered, swept away by currents or dragged under by things that moved in the murk.

By the time Venn found dry ground, she was alone.

She followed the needle’s pulse west. Or thought she did. But the delta distorted everything. She walked for many days before she found the others.

They were about a dozen, huddled in the roots of a massive mangrove that had grown around what might once have been a stone foundation. Their leader, a woman named Ria, had organized them into watches, but they were barely surviving. The giants here were different—smaller, faster, adapted to the channels. They moved through the water with a silence that made them invisible until they struck.

“We lost several in the first week,” Ria said. “They come at night. Or during high tide. We’ve learned to climb, to stay in the high branches where they can’t reach. But we can’t build here.”

Venn looked at the survivors. Thin. Hollow. The needle’s pulse was a distant memory.

“There’s a place,” Venn said. “East. The pulse is stronger there. The Keepers said the needle would protect us.”

“The needle is broken,” Ria said flatly. “It’s dying.”

“It’s still there.”

After a few more days, a giant found their refuge.

It came at dusk, moving through the channels with a predator’s patience. The sentry saw it first, but before she could cry out, the giant’s arm swept up and smashed the branch she was standing on. She fell screaming, and the water closed over her.

The others scattered, climbing higher. The giant circled below, its stony hide scraping against the trunk. It was smaller than the swamp giants—perhaps twenty feet—but in the tight confines of the delta, it was unstoppable.

Venn pressed herself against a branch, her heart pounding. Below, the giant’s hand closed around a root and pulled. The whole structure groaned.

“We have to hit it,” she said. “Together. Use the vrill.”

“We can’t shape stone. There’s no stone here.”

“Then we shape the wood. The water. We shape what we have.”

She reached down into the vrill current that flowed through the delta—faint, unstable, but present—and pressed her will into the branch beneath her. It was like trying to catch water with bare hands. The vrill slipped through her fingers.

Below, the giant pulled again. The mangrove shrieked.

“Now!” Venn shouted.

The branch didn’t shape. But it moved—a fraction, a twist. Around her, others were doing the same, their combined will finding purchase in the unstable current. Branches shifted, roots writhed. A branch the size of a man’s torso swung down and caught the giant across the head.

It didn’t fall. But it staggered. And in that moment, Venn saw something she hadn’t expected: fear.

“Again!”

The survivors threw their will into the vrill, and the mangrove answered. Branches lashed, roots coiled, and the giant retreated, splashing back into the channel.

At dawn, Venn found the sentry’s body floating in the channel, already dissolving into vrill-ash.

“We leave today,” she said.

VIII. The Fire Mountain Group

Far to the south, where the continent fractured into a labyrinth of volcanic vents and obsidian cliffs, a different gathering was taking place.

Renn had landed badly.

His thought-seed had torn through the Veil with a violence that left him unconscious for many hours, his body half-formed and bleeding vrill into the scorched earth. When he finally woke, he was alone. The crater around him was filled with ash and shattered crystal, and the sky above was the color of iron.

He had followed the pulse north—or what he thought was north. The needle’s signal was weaker here, distorted by the volcanic activity, but he could feel it: a distant heartbeat, calling him toward stability. He walked for a day before he found the others.

They were a handful, huddled in a cave system that cut through the volcanic rock. Their leader was a Nephoretti named Deryn, who had landed with a small group. Several hadn’t survived the first night.

“The giants found us before we could find shelter,” Deryn said. Her voice was raw, her arm bandaged with torn cloth. “They came out of the steam. We didn’t even see them until…”

She didn’t finish. Renn didn’t need her to. The marks were everywhere—clawed stone, shattered crystals, the faint vrill-ash that marked where a Nephoretti had dissolved back into raw potential.

“There are more of us,” Renn said. “North. Near the needle. I felt it before the volcanoes started interfering.”

Deryn looked at her remaining companions—half a dozen, exhausted, injured, their geomancy too weak to shape the hostile stone around them. “We can’t make that journey. The giants patrol the lowlands. And the terrain between here and the needle…” She shook her head.

They spent the next several days in the caves, surviving on what little vrill they could draw from the volcanic vents. The giants came twice, their massive forms silhouetted against the smoke, their stony faces expressionless. The Nephoretti hid in the deepest crevices, pressing themselves into the rock, willing themselves to be silent.

Within only a few days in the caves, they heard singing.

It was faint, carried on the wind from somewhere to the north. Not Nephoretti voices—something deeper, more resonant. The giants heard it too. They turned toward the sound, their massive heads cocked, and for a long moment they stood motionless. Then, as one, they began to move east, away from the caves, away from the Nephoretti.

“What was that?” Deryn whispered.

Renn didn’t know. But when the giants had disappeared into the steam, he led them out of the caves and began the journey north. They moved at night, when the volcanic glare was muted and the giants were less active. They lost one more—a Nephoretti who slipped on an obsidian slope and fell into a crevasse too deep to reach.

By the time they reached the lowlands, their group had grown to nearly a dozen. Other survivors had emerged from the cracks and craters, drawn by the same instinct, following the same distant pulse. They moved together, silent and watchful, learning to read the land: where the giants hunted, where the ground was stable, where the vrill currents pooled in pockets of relative calm.

The days seemed endless, the terrain hostile, and then finally on the horizon, the needle came into view.

It rose from the swamp like a broken bone, its upper reaches lost in the haze. Around its base, lights flickered—not the erratic pulse of a damaged system, but something steadier. Something organized.

“They’ve built,” Deryn said, and there was wonder in her voice.

As they approached the ancient plaza, they saw the wall, the shelters, the Nephoretti moving with purpose. And at the center, standing at the needle’s base, a woman with dark hair and steady eyes watched them come.

Teryn counted them as they entered: a handful of survivors, worn and wounded, their faces carrying the weight of what they had endured. Behind them, more figures emerged from the swamp—stragglers from other groups, drawn by the same beacon.

“We’re not all here,” Renn said when he reached her. His voice cracked from days of silence. “There are others. South. West. Scattered. Some didn’t make it.”

Teryn nodded. She had expected this. The Keepers had sent a thousand, and more than half had survived the jump. Of those, perhaps half would find their way here. The rest were out there, alone, surviving or not.

“We’ll find them,” she said. “We’ll build, and we’ll find them.”

IX. The Thread

Several weeks later, a scout from the needle found Venn’s delta group.

They had built rafts from mangrove wood, lashing the trunks together with vines, using vrill to seal the gaps. It was crude work, but it held. About a dozen survivors had made the journey, poling through the channels, hiding when giants passed, following the pulse that grew stronger with each mile.

The scout—a young Nephoretti named Corin—found them at the edge of the lowlands, where the delta gave way to firmer ground. He had been sent west days ago, following the same pulse that had guided Venn’s group.

“There are many of us,” he said, looking at the ragged survivors. “At the needle. You’re safe now.”

Venn wanted to believe him. But as they walked the final miles, as the broken spire rose against the sky and the pulse steadied beneath her feet, she felt something else: a presence, vast and patient, waiting in the stone. Not a threat. Not a promise. Just… waiting.

She found Teryn at the plaza’s edge, watching the new arrivals with quiet eyes.

“We lost many,” Venn said. “On the landing, and after. The rest are behind us—some chose to stay, to bring others. But most didn’t make it.”

Teryn nodded. “We’ll find them. Or they’ll find us. The pulse calls them home.”

Venn looked at the needle, at the light flickering in its broken core. “Is that what it is? A home?”

“It’s what we make it.” Teryn turned to look at the settlement taking shape around them. “The Keepers sent us here to build. So we build.”

In the days that followed, Venn’s survivors were absorbed into the growing community. They brought knowledge of the delta—its currents, its dangers, the smaller giants that hunted there. They brought stories of others still out there, scattered across the western reaches, waiting for rescue.

The scouts continued their work, mapping the land, marking safe routes, bringing back survivors in twos and threes. By the time the first season had passed, the population at the needle had grown to a couple hundred. The wall had become a fortress, the shelters a small town. And the pulse—the pulse was steadier now, stronger, as if the presence of so many living voices had given it something to hold onto.

No one understood why. But they didn’t question it. Not yet.

X. The Grove Giants

As the season turned, the scouts returned with reports of a new kind of giant in the highlands—smaller than the swamp giants, perhaps twenty feet, but faster. More organized. They used tools: stone axes, battering rams. They worked together in flanking maneuvers. And they were herding survivors, pinning them in caves rather than killing them outright.

“We need to bring them in,” Teryn said. “Renn, you came through the highlands. Take a small group. Fast, quiet. Find them and guide them back.”

Renn’s group left at dusk—half a dozen volunteers, moving like shadows through the western marsh. They carried nothing but crystal shards from the needle, pulse‑anchors that would guide them back.

The journey took several days. The terrain shifted from swamp to rocky foothills, then to a landscape of black basalt and sulfurous steam vents that hissed like angry serpents. Here, the needle’s pulse was a ghost, barely perceptible, and the vrill currents ran wild and unpredictable.

They found the survivors on the fourth morning—about thirty Nephoretti huddled in a cave system that wound deep into the volcanic rock. Their leader was a woman named Nessa, whose sharp features and steady eyes spoke of someone who had learned to make hard decisions.

“We thought we were the only ones,” she said. “We saw the lights in the sky when you landed, but the giants—” She gestured toward the cave entrance, where the distant shape of a grove giant could be seen patrolling the valley below. “They didn’t let us leave.”

“How many?” Renn asked.

“Started with several dozen. Lost a number in the first week to the giants. The rest… we lost to the caves. To the heat. To not knowing which way was safe.”

Nessa led him to a crevice that overlooked the valley. Below, a grove of stone trees rose from the volcanic soil—not living trees, but formations that mimicked the shape of ancient oaks, their trunks fused with crystal. And moving among them, a giant.

It was smaller than the swamp giants—perhaps twenty feet—but its body was denser, more compact. Its hide was the color of cooled lava, shot through with veins of obsidian that caught the morning light. In its hands, it carried a stone axe, the blade knapped from volcanic glass with a precision that spoke of intelligence, not instinct.

“There are a few of them,” Nessa whispered. “They take turns patrolling. When one leaves, another comes. They’ve been doing it for weeks.”

“And they haven’t attacked?”

“They don’t need to. They’re waiting for us to come out. Every time we tried to leave, they drove us back. Not killing—herding. Like they’re… corralling us.”

Renn watched the grove giant move through the stone forest, its movements deliberate, patient. This was not the mindless aggression of the swamp giants. This was hunting with purpose.

“We’re not leaving the way we came,” he said. “We go through the caves. There has to be another exit.”

It took them several more days to find a route through the volcanic tunnels, following the faintest pulses of vrill that seeped through cracks in the rock. They emerged on the far side of the grove, in a ravine that led down toward the lowlands. About thirty survivors, all exhausted, all terrified, but alive.

They traveled by night, avoiding the patrols, moving in absolute silence. After many nights, they felt it: the needle’s pulse, faint but unmistakable, calling them home.

Behind them, the grove giants did not follow. But Renn had seen the way they watched, the way they communicated with gestures and sounds that were almost language. They were not mindless. They were waiting, and when the Nephoretti returned to the highlands—as they eventually would—the grove giants would be ready.

XI. The Cliffs

While Renn’s group made their slow way back to the needle, another drama was unfolding far to the east, where the continent dropped away into sheer cliffs overlooking a churning gray sea.

Mira had landed on a narrow ledge high above the water, her thought‑seed slamming into the rock with enough force to fracture it. She had lain there for a long while, her body slowly solidifying, before she could move.

She was alone.

The pulse from the needle was faint here, distorted by the iron-rich cliffs and the constant crash of waves. But it was there—a thread she could follow, if she could just reach stable ground. The problem was the cliffs themselves. She had climbed rock faces in the memory‑seeds The Keepers had given her, but those were memories, not experience. Her hands were bleeding before she’d climbed very far.

After a few days, she heard something above her.

Voices. Not Nephoretti—something deeper, more resonant. She pressed herself against the rock, heart pounding, and listened. The sounds were rhythmic, almost like chanting, and they seemed to come from a ledge somewhere above.

She climbed toward them.

What she found was a cave opening, wide enough to admit a giant, its entrance worn smooth by ages of use. Inside, a fire burned—actual fire, fed by driftwood and something that gave off a sweet, smoky scent. And around the fire, several figures.

They were Nephoretti. A handful of women and a man, their bioluminescence dim but steady, their bodies whole.

“We saw you fall,” said the eldest, a woman with silver‑grey skin and eyes that had seen too much. “We’ve been watching ever since. Hoping you’d find us.”

Her name was Ione, and she had landed with several dozen. About half had survived the first week. The rest had been taken by the cliffs, by the cold, by the creatures that hunted the tidal pools. Now they were a handful—half a dozen, counting Mira.

“We can’t stay here,” Ione said. “The winter storms are coming. We’ve seen them before. The waves will reach this cave within a season. We need to go inland, find the others.”

Mira looked at the survivors. They were tough—the cliffs had made them so—but they were also few. Half a dozen against the journey to the needle, through terrain they didn’t know, with giants that might be anywhere.

“Then we go now,” she said. “We follow the coast until we find a way up. Then we find the pulse and we follow it home.”

It took them nearly two weeks. They climbed cliffs, crossed beaches patrolled by giants that moved like shadows, hid in caves when the storms came. They lost a couple more—one to a fall, one to a giant that emerged from the mist before they could react. But the others pressed on, driven by the pulse that grew stronger with each mile.

When they finally crested the last ridge and saw the needle rising from the swamp, its broken spire catching the sunset, Mira felt something she hadn’t allowed herself since landing: hope.

Below, in the plaza, lights flickered. Voices rose in the evening work‑song, and the needle’s pulse seemed to beat in answer.

XII. Convergence

By the time the season had fully settled, the settlement at the needle had grown to several hundred survivors.

Teryn stood with Kael and Renn on the wall, watching the latest arrivals make their way up from the eastern trail. Ragged, thin, their bioluminescence barely visible—but alive.

“The others are out there,” Renn said. “The highlands group told me about survivors to the west. And Ione’s people said there were clusters along the southern coast.”

“We’ll find them,” Teryn said. “Or they’ll find us. The pulse calls them home.”

She looked out at the darkening landscape, at the line of ancient stones that marked the boundary the giants never crossed. Beyond that line, the giants were gathering. The big ones from the swamp, the grove giants from the highlands, the cliff giants from the east—all watching, waiting, learning.

The southern continent was not a garden waiting to be cultivated. It was a war of territories, each giant clan defending its land, its way of life.

“We need to understand them,” she said. “The big ones attack. The grove ones herd. The cliff ones… we don’t even know. But they’re not the same. They’re not unified.”

“That could work for us,” Renn said. “Or against us.”

Teryn nodded slowly. “We need to find one clan we can reach. One that might see us not as prey, but as something else.”

She thought of the way the pulse responded to sound, to the work‑songs that had become part of the settlement’s daily rhythm. The way the giants at the boundary sometimes paused when the singing rose.

“Renn, you said the grove giants use tools. They herd rather than kill. That’s strategy. That’s thought.”

“They’re smarter than the swamp ones,” he agreed. “Or at least more patient.”

“Then maybe they’re the ones we need to find. Not to fight—to talk.”

Kael looked at her sharply. “You want to go into the highlands? After what happened to Nessa’s group?”

“Not yet. But someday.” She turned to face them, the needle’s pulse vibrating through her feet, the voices of the settlement rising in the evening air. “We’re not here to conquer. We’re here to build. But you can’t build a garden in a war zone. First we need peace. Or at least a truce.”

She looked up at the broken spire, at the light flickering in its damaged core. Somewhere in that ancient structure, something was listening. Something that responded to sound, to harmony, to the voices of those who sought to create rather than destroy. And deeper still, in the darkness she had glimpsed on that first night, something else was waking—patient, unknown, waiting for the right moment to stir.

The Second Song had begun. But it would not reach its full voice until they found others to sing it with.
