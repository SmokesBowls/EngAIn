The morning light was still new when Vale returned to the wall.

He had not slept—he did not know if he could sleep, or if sleep was something he would ever need. But he had sat at the base of the needle through the night, listening to the pulse, feeling the city breathe around him. Now he stood at the eastern parapet, his hands pressed to the stone, and watched the sun climb over the marsh.

Granite found him there, as he had the day before. The old giant moved slowly now, its wounds from the war still healing, but its eyes were sharp.

“You did not rest.”

“I do not know if I need to.”

Granite made a sound that might have been amusement. “None of us know what you need. That is part of the strangeness.”

Vale turned from the sun. “You have questions.”

“We all have questions.” Granite leaned its massive form against the wall, its crystal patterns dim in the morning light. “The council meets this afternoon. They will ask you what you intend to do. What you are. What you want.”

“And what do you think I should tell them?”

Granite was silent for a moment. “I think you should tell them the truth. Whatever that is.”

“I do not know the truth yet. I don't  know what I was. I do not know what I will become.”

“Then tell them that.” Granite looked at the Ma'at, at the dark shape on the horizon. “They have waited six thousand years for you to wake. They can wait a little longer for answers.”

The council met in the hall that had been built on the site of the old plaza, its walls carved with the history of Garden Grove. Mora sat at the head of the table, her face calm, her hands steady. Around her sat the leaders of the city—the crafters, the farmers, the scouts, the giants who had fought beside them through the Second Shadow.

Vale stood at the center of the room, his form flickering between solid and insubstantial, his presence making the air feel heavy.

“We have questions,” Mora said. Her voice was gentle, but there was steel beneath it. “You have been awake for a day. You have walked our walls, touched our needle, spoken with our people. But we do not know what you are, or what you intend.”

“I do not know what I am,” Vale said. The words were vibration, felt in the stone beneath their feet. "I feel as I was left to sleep while...  the world changed around me. And then you sang to me. For six thousand years, you sang.”

 II. Day Two: The Currents

Damu found him at the needle’s base before dawn.

She was old—older than any of them—and her movements were slow, deliberate. But her eyes were sharp, and her hands were steady as she pressed a crystal shard into his palm.

“Yesterday you saw the colors,” she said. “Today you learn to read them.”

She led him to the eastern wall, where the sun was beginning to paint the sky in shades of gold and copper. The marsh lay below them, the channels running clear, the reeds green for the first time in three hundred years. And in the distance, the Ma'at pulsed with its slow, heavy rhythm.

“Close your eyes,” Damu said. “Reach out. Tell me what you see.”

He did. The world opened around him—not as sight, not as sound, but as currents. One thread flowed through the marsh like slow rivers, purple and gold, patient and deep. And a second thread currents coiled around the Ma'at like serpents, black and silver, waiting. And between them, threads of green, blue, red—the colors of life, of will, of the world choosing itself.

“I see the purple and gold,” he said. “It is slow. Slower than it was yesterday.”

“Interesting. Vrill deep essence, What else?”

He looked deeper. He found the channels where the water moved, the fields where the crops grew, the walls where the stone had been shaped by generations of geomancy. And beneath it all, he found something else—a current that was not only vrill by itself, but something that moved between them.

“There is something else,” he said. “Something black and silver, another thread, and something that is both. Something that is neither.”

Damu nodded slowly. “That is what the Igigi have been trying to understand for three thousand years. The vrill and the void are supposed to be opposites. Enemies. But here, in this place, they are learning to coexist. To become something new.”

“Vrill and void? Because of the songs?.”

“Because of the songs. Because of the needle. Because of six thousand years of a world that refused to break.” She pressed another shard into his hand. “Now. Tell me what you see in the Ma'at.”

He hesitated. The Ma'at was a knot of discord, a wound in the fabric that had been festering for seven thousand years. He had felt it the first time he opened his eyes, had heard its voice in the silence between the beats.

He reached for it.

The currents around the Ma'at were different. The vrill did not flow there—it was blocked, nullified, pushed back by the void resonance that the swamp giants had built. And the void itself was twisted, wrong, shaped by hands that did not understand what they were shaping.

But beneath it, beneath the discord, there was something else. A thread. Thin, almost invisible, but there. A thread that connected the Ma'at to the needle.

“There is a connection,” he said. “Between the Ma'at and the needle. A thread of… something. Something that should not be there.”

Damu’s face was unreadable. “We have seen it. The Igigi have been measuring it for three hundred years. It is why the Ma'at’s rhythm mimics the needle. Why the swamp giants have not been able to cut themselves off completely.”

“What is it?”

“We do not know. But it seems to be pulling from the same well.. void and vrill create a veil.. inside that viel is the Ethereal... we have measured it against the Ethereal.. it's not the same.. and there wasn't much about the gravatons knowledge that went farther then their own minds.. "

Vale looked at the Ma'at, at the dark shape on the horizon, at the thread that connected it to the needle beneath his feet. A whole web of intricate conflicts and the one thing that occupied his mind, the gravitons... He looked up to damu and thought to ask, but something kept him from wanting to say the word out loud....And for the first time, he understood that the war was not just about him. It was about something older. Something that had been started before the 7 millenia... Before the singing woke him up.

 III. Day Three: The Synergy

Mora came to him at midday.

The sun was high, the light warm, the marsh alive with the sound of work. She found him in the outer complex, where the grove giants were rebuilding the walls that had been damaged in the war. He had been helping—not with his hands, which were still uncertain, but with his presence. Where he stood, the stone grew lighter. Where he touched, the cracks sealed themselves.

“You are learning,” she said.

“I am learning.”

“The council has decided.” She sat on a block of stone that had been waiting to be shaped, her hands resting on her knees. “We want you to stay. To learn. To become what you are becoming. And when you are ready, we want you to help us end the war.”

“The war with the swamp giants.”

“Yes.” She looked at the Ma'at, at the dark shape on the horizon. “They have been our enemies for seven thousand years."

Vale sat beside her, his form settling into something more solid. “And what is it that you are expecting.”

“I want to give them a choice.” She turned to him, her eyes sharp, her face set. “The same choice you are making. The same choice we have made, every day, for seven thousand years. To become something more than what we were made to be.”

He looked at her for a long moment. He thought of the songs that had fed him, the voices that had sustained him, the world that had waited for him to wake. He thought of the thread that connected the Ma'at to the needle, the connection that should not exist, the something that was pulling at both ends.

“I will help,” he said. “But not yet. I am not ready. And neither are they.”

Mora nodded slowly. “Then we will wait. And we will work. And when you are ready, we will go together.”

She stood, and for a moment she looked like her grandmother, her great-grandmother, all the women who had stood on this wall and watched the east.

“The sun is warm,” she said. “The fields are growing. The children are laughing. For the first time in three hundred years, we are not just surviving. We are living.”

She looked at him.

“That is what we are fighting for. Not to destroy the Ma'at. Not to defeat the swamp giants. To live. To grow. To become something that was not planned.”

She walked away, leaving him on the wall, watching the sun set over the marsh.

 IV. The First Week

The days that followed were a blur of learning.

Vale walked the walls with Granite, learning the rhythms of the war that had shaped the marsh for three centuries. He sat with Enlil in the workshops, learning the mathematics of the needle, the slow healing of the crystal veins, the work that remained. He sang with the Nephoretti in the fields, his voice—vibration, not sound—weaving through their songs, finding harmonies he had not known existed.

He learned that his body was not a body—it was a question he was asking of the world, a demand that space bend around him, that gravity acknowledge his presence, that matter decide where he ended and it began. When he walked, he left no footprints. When he touched stone, the stone remembered him. When he stood still, the air around him grew heavy, as if the world was holding its breath.

He learned that he could see the currents—the vrill flowing through the marsh like slow rivers, the void coiling around the Ma'at like serpents, the threads of life and will that moved between them. He learned to read them, to measure them, to understand the shape of the world that had been built in his absence.

And every morning, he stood on the eastern wall and watched the sun rise over the Ma'at.

The Ma'at did not change. Its crystal veins pulsed with their slow, heavy rhythm, unchanged for seven thousand years. The swamp giants moved on its slopes, their forms dark against the light, their eyes always watching. The nullification zones—the places where his influence could not reach—still lay like wounds in the marsh.

But he did not try to heal them. Not yet.

He was learning. And in the long days that stretched ahead, he would learn to become something that none of them had expected.

 V. The First Month

The city grew accustomed to him.

The children stopped staring. The adults stopped whispering. The giants stopped watching him with the wariness of creatures who had fought for three hundred years and were not sure the war was over.

He became part of the rhythm—the morning songs, the midday work, the evening gatherings at the base of the needle. He learned names, faces, stories. He learned the history of Garden Grove, the long watch, the slow building of a city that had refused to break.

And he learned that he was not alone.

The voice from the Ma'at did not speak again. But he felt it there, waiting. The thread that connected the Ma'at to the needle pulled at him, a constant presence at the edge of his awareness. Something was watching. Something was waiting.

He was not ready. Not yet.

But he was learning.

And in the years ahead, he would learn to become something that none of them had expected.

The light had returned. The war was not over. But for the first time in seven thousand years, something was changing in the east.

Vale stood on the wall and watched the sun rise, and in the silence between the beats, he listened.

 VI. Day Thirty – The Thread

The morning light had settled into something ordinary.

That was the strangest part, Vale thought—how quickly the extraordinary became familiar. The sun rose, the city woke, the songs began. What had been a miracle on the first day was now the shape of things. He stood on the eastern wall and watched the Ma'at pulse in the distance, and it was simply what he did.

Granite found him there, as it did most mornings. The old giant's wounds were healing, its movements less labored, but its eyes remained fixed on the east with the patience of something that had been watching for centuries.

"The thread," Granite said. "You still feel it."

"Yes."

"Has it changed?"

Vale closed his eyes. He reached for the connection—the thin line of something that was neither vrill nor void, that ran from the needle to the Ma'at like a scar that had never healed. It was still there. Always there.

"It is the same. Constant. Waiting."

Granite made a sound that might have been acknowledgment. "The swamp giants felt it too. That is why they built the Ma'at. To block it. To contain it. They thought if they built high enough, they could cut themselves off from whatever was pulling at them."

"They were wrong."

"They were desperate." Granite looked at the Ma'at, its crystal patterns dim. "Desperation does not care about right or wrong. Only about surviving until tomorrow."

Vale considered this. He had been desperate once—trapped in the stone, the pulse weakening, the songs growing thin. He had not cared about right or wrong. He had only wanted to hold on.

"The thread is not a weapon," he said. "It is not a trap. It is something else."

"What?"

"I do not know yet."

Day Forty-Five — The Children

They came to him in the afternoon, when the sun was warm and the work in the fields had paused.

Three of them—thought‑seeds, children by Nephoretti measure, though they had been alive for decades by the reckoning of other worlds. They stood at the edge of the outer complex, watching him with the particular intensity of the young who have been told a story and are trying to match it to the thing standing before them.

The boldest one stepped forward. A boy, his hair the color of copper, his hands stained with the dye from the crafters' quarter.

"Are you really a Graviton?" he asked.

"I don't know entirely what one of these are, would it be bad if I was one"

The boy thought and looked down. "I don't know either, but I think they make magic, can you make magic too"

"I don't think it quite works like that, no I can't"

"Are you really the one who was sleeping in the needle?"

"I was."

"Did you hear my grandmother sing? Her name was Sephra. She sang every morning."

Vale knelt—a movement that was becoming more natural, his form holding steady for longer stretches. "I heard her. Her voice was the first thing I knew, after the pulse."

The boy's face did something complicated. Pride and grief and wonder, all tangled together. "She said you would wake. She said when the light came back, you would be here."

"She was right."

The other children stepped closer. A girl with dark braids and sharp eyes. Another boy, smaller, his hands wrapped around a crystal shard that pulsed with faint light.

"What does it feel like?" the girl asked. "Being in the stone for so long?"

Vale thought about it. He had not let himself think about it—the long darkness, the slow fading, the threads of song that had held him together when everything else was falling apart.

"It felt like drowning," he said. "For a very long time. And then it felt like being held."

The children were quiet. The smaller boy held his crystal shard tighter.

"Are you going to fight the swamp giants?" the copper-haired boy asked.

Vale looked at the Ma'at, at the dark shape on the horizon. He thought of the thread that connected it to the needle, the desperation that had built it, the voice that had spoken to him in the silence between beats.

"I am going to understand them," he said. "And then I will decide."

Day Sixty — The Workshop

Enlil had a new project.

The Igigi's workshop was a maze of crystal shards and measuring tools, maps that spanned centuries, calculations that filled walls. Vale had learned to navigate it, to read the patterns, to understand the slow accumulation of knowledge that had kept Garden Grove alive through the grey.

Now Enlil stood at a table covered in fragments—pieces of the Ma'at's crystal, brought back by scouts who had risked the nullification zones. He held one up to the light, his long fingers tracing its edges.

"They are learning," he said. "The swamp giants. Their resonance is changing."

Vale took the fragment. It was cold, wrong, its void signature twisted in ways that made his own currents shift uncomfortably. But beneath the discord, there was something else. A pattern. A shape.

"They are trying to make it permanent," he said. "The nullification. They want it to be part of them, not just something they build."

Enlil nodded slowly. "If they succeed, they will be immune to the needle's influence. To your influence. The war will become something new. Something we cannot predict."

"Can they succeed?"

"I do not know." Enlil's face was tired, older than it had been when Vale woke. "The Igigi have been studying the Ma'at for three hundred years. We understand its structure, its resonance, its weaknesses. But we do not understand what is driving it. What is pulling at it."

"The thread."

"Yes." Enlil looked at him. "You feel it too. The connection between the needle and the Ma'at. It should not exist. The vrill and the graviton are opposites. Enemies. They should repel each other."

"They do not."

"No." Enlil's voice was quiet. "And that is what frightens me. Something is holding them together."

Vale looked at the crystal fragment, at the twisted resonance that the swamp giants were trying to make their own. He thought of the thread, the constant presence at the edge of his awareness, the voice that had spoken once and then gone silent.

"When the scouts go again," he said, "I want to go with them."

Enlil's eyes widened. "The nullification zones—"

"I can endure them. I was nullified for six thousand years. A few hours in the marsh will not break me."

"That is not what I fear." Enlil set the crystal down carefully, as if it might break. "I fear what you will find. What the thread will do when you get close to it."

Vale considered this. He considered the voice, the silence, the weight of something that had been waiting for seven thousand years.

"If I do not go," he said, "I will never understand. And if I do not understand, I will never be anything but what I was."

Day Seventy-Five — The Song Circle

They gathered at the base of the needle as the sun set.

It was not a council, not a celebration. It was simply what they did now—the Nephoretti and the grove giants and the Igigi, sitting together in the fading light, their voices rising in the songs that had sustained the city for millennia.

Vale sat at the edge of the circle, his form steady, his hands pressed to the stone. He did not sing—his voice was vibration, not sound, and it unsettled some of them when he used it. But he listened. And as he listened, he felt the pulse shift.

It was subtle—a quickening, a warmth that spread through the crystal veins. The songs were feeding him, the way they had for six thousand years. But now he was outside the stone, separate from the pulse, and the feeling was different. It was not survival anymore. It was something closer to gratitude.

Mora sat beside him, her voice steady in the harmony. When the song ended, she turned to him.

"You are learning to be with us."

"I am learning."

She looked at the needle, at the light pulsing in its core, at the city that had grown in its shadow. "My grandmother used to say that the songs were not just for the Keeper. They were for us. To remind us that we were still here. Still choosing to be here."

"She was right."

"She usually was." Mora smiled, but there was something behind it—a weight, a question she had been holding for a long time. "Do you remember it? Before the songs? Before the needle?"

Vale was quiet for a moment. He had not let himself remember. The memories were there, fragments of a life that was not his anymore. A world of fire and gravity, of orders given and followed, of a purpose that had been written into him before he had ever chosen anything.

"I remember being restraint," he said. "I remember being elevated by the singing. And I don't remember but when I first stepped out of the stone and the song stopped.. my first thoughs were of being a weapon, flashes of destruction to a place I don't remember. I could not stop thinking of what I might have meant to be."

"And now?"

He looked at his hands—steady now, more solid than they had been on the first day. He thought of the songs, the voices, the long centuries of being held in the dark.

"Now I am learning to choose."

Day Ninety — The Edge

He went to the boundary alone.

The sun was high, the light gold on the marsh, and the nullification zones lay ahead like shadows on the water. Vale stood at the line where the spiers influence faded, where the pulse became a rumor, where the world began to feel wrong.

He stepped forward.

The change was immediate—a pressure, a silence, a weight that pressed against him from all sides. The currents that he had learned to read vanished, replaced by a static that made his thoughts feel slow, his form uncertain.

He kept walking.

The marsh around him was different here. The reeds were grey, the water dark, the channels choked with silt that had not been cleared in centuries. The air was cold, even in the sunlight, and the silence was absolute.

He walked for an hour. Two hours. The pressure built, the static thickened, and he felt himself beginning to fray—the edges of his form blurring, the currents that held him together weakening.

Then he felt it.

The thread. Close now, close enough to touch. It pulsed with the same rhythm as the needle, the same rhythm as his own heart, and in its pulse he heard the voice again.

You came.

He stopped. The marsh was still, the silence complete, and he was alone with the thread and the voice and the weight of something that had been waiting for seven thousand years.

"I came to understand," he said.

Then understand. The Ma'at is not your enemy. It is your reflection. What you were, before the songs. What you could become again, if you let go.

He felt the pull then—the thread tightening, drawing him toward the Ma'at, toward the darkness that pulsed at its core. The static pressed against him, and for a moment he felt the old purpose rising, the hunger for destruction that had been written into him before he had ever chosen anything.

He planted his feet. The marsh mud sucked at him, but he held.

"I am not what I was."

You are what you were made to be. The songs are a wound, not a healing. They have made you weak. Soft. If you let go, you could be strong again. Whole.

"I don't know what I was, I don't know if I was strong.. but I know that strength is not mine now.. so what would the strength make me if I took it?"

"what do you want to be?"
The question hung in the air, the same question Mora had asked him on the third day. He had not had an answer then. He was not sure he had one now.

But he knew what he did not want.

"I don't want to be something that already is, that was," he said. "I want to be something that I can become on my own."

The thread pulsed. The pressure eased, just slightly.

Then you will have to earn it. The Ma'at will not fall because you wish it. The swamp giants will not change because you ask. If you want to be something new, you will have to become it. Slowly. Painfully. Over centuries.

"I have time."

We all have time. That is the curse of being what we are.

The voice faded. The thread loosened, drawing back toward the Ma'at, and Vale felt the pressure lift. He stood in the nullification zone, alone in the silence, and let himself breathe.

He was not ready. He knew that now. Not ready to face the Ma'at, not ready to end the war, not ready to become what he was trying to become.

But he was learning.

He turned and walked back toward the needle, toward the light, toward the city that was waiting for him. Behind him, the Ma'at pulsed with its slow, heavy rhythm, unchanged for seven thousand years.

But something had shifted. Something small. Something that might, in time, become a beginning.

I — The First Century: Learning to Be

The decades after Vale’s waking passed like water over stone—slow, patient, shaping without haste.

He did not become a leader. He did not become a weapon. He became something the city had not expected: a student. For years he sat with the Igigi, learning to read the currents that flowed through marsh and stone. He walked the walls with Granite, learning the rhythms of the war that had ended but not finished. He sang with the Nephoretti, his voice—vibration, not sound—finding harmonies that the old songs had not contained.

His body grew steadier. The flickering that had marked his first days faded to an occasional shimmer at the edges. He learned to leave footprints when he wanted to, to cast a shadow, to sit in the sun without the air around him growing heavy. He was still not quite solid—he might never be—but he was present in a way that the city could hold.

The Ma'at remained on the horizon, unchanging. Its pulse was a constant, a second heartbeat that Vale felt even when he did not listen for it. The thread that connected it to the needle thrummed with the same tension, neither tightening nor loosening. The voice did not speak again, but he felt its presence—waiting, watching, measuring the slow accumulation of his becoming.

The swamp giants did not attack. They had withdrawn to the Ma'at after the last battle, their numbers reduced, their confidence shattered. Scouts reported that they were rebuilding, not for war but for something else. New terraces rose on the Ma'at’s slopes. Crystal veins were reshaped into patterns that had not been there before. They were not fortifying. They were transforming.

“They are learning,” Granite said one evening, as they stood together on the eastern wall. The old giant had grown older, its movements slower, its crystal patterns dimming. “The same way we learned. The same way you are learning.”

“What are they learning to become?”

Granite looked at the Ma'at, at the dark shape against the sunset. “Something that can live with what they are. Something that does not need to fight.”

II — The Second Century: The Garden Grows

Garden Grove expanded. The fields that had been planted in the first year of the returned sun now stretched to the western hills, their yields abundant. The channels that Nin had mapped were dredged and widened, their waters clear. The outer complex grew into a district of its own, its streets wide enough for giants, its buildings shaped by generations of Nephoretti geomancy and Igigi calculation.

Children were born—thought‑seeds who had never known the grey, who grew up in the light and took it for granted. They learned the old songs in the schools, but they did not understand them. The grey was a story, not a memory. The war was something their grandparents spoke of in the winter gatherings.

Vale became part of the landscape. He was the one who helped lift the heavy stones when the walls were extended. He was the one who sat with the crafters when the crystal veins needed shaping. He was the one who walked the marsh when the nullification zones shifted, mapping their edges, learning their patterns.

He did not try to enter the zones again. The memory of that first walk—the pressure, the silence, the voice—stayed with him. He was not ready. But he was patient.

In the second century, the first child was born who could see the currents the way he saw them.

Her name was Elara, and she was the daughter of a crafters and a scout. When she was six, she pointed at the needle and said, “It’s singing purple today.” Her parents brought her to Vale.

He knelt beside her, his form steady, his voice soft. “What else do you see?”

She frowned, concentrating. “There’s a string. A thin one. It goes east.” She pointed at the Ma'at.

Vale felt the thread pulse, once, as if acknowledging her. “That string has been there for a very long time.”

“Can I learn to see more?”

“Yes,” he said. “And I will teach you.”

III — The Third Century: The Thread Tightens

The changes came slowly, so slowly that at first no one noticed.

The Ma'at’s rhythm shifted—not faster or slower, but deeper, as if something beneath it was waking. The nullification zones expanded, not toward Garden Grove but sideways, linking together, forming a web that spread through the eastern marsh. The swamp giants worked without rest, their songs rising day and night, their crystal veins pulsing with a light that was no longer just black and silver but threaded with something new.

Vale felt it before the scouts reported it. The thread that connected the needle to the Ma'at had tightened. It was not a weapon, not yet, but it was no longer just a scar. It was becoming something deliberate.

Elara, now a woman of eighty years, stood with him on the eastern wall. Her gift had grown over the decades; she could see the currents as clearly as he could, could trace the web that the swamp giants were weaving.

“They are not blocking us anymore,” she said. “They are building something. Something that will let them move through the zones without losing themselves.”

“What do you see?”

She closed her eyes, reaching. “They are learning to carry the nullification with them. To make it part of their bodies. If they succeed, they will be able to walk anywhere. Even here.”

Vale looked at the Ma'at. He had been waiting for this—for the moment when the swamp giants’ desperation would drive them to something new. He had hoped it would be a reaching out, a negotiation. But this was something else. This was a hardening.

“Can you reach them?” he asked. “Through the thread?”

Elara was silent for a long moment. “I can try.”

She sang. Her voice was not the voice of the old songs—it was something newer, something that had learned to shape both vrill and void. The thread pulsed, and for a moment Vale felt the presence on the other end—not the voice that had spoken to him, but something older, something that had been in the Ma'at since before the grey.

Then the thread snapped back, and Elara gasped.

“They are afraid,” she said. “They know we are watching. They do not want us to see what they are building.”

“What are they building?”

She shook her head. “I cannot see. But it is not just for defense. It is for something they have been waiting for. Something that is coming.”

IV — The Fourth Century: The Sky Changes

The signs began in the sky.

It was subtle—a shimmer at the edge of the atmosphere, a distortion in the light that only the most sensitive could see. Vale felt it before he saw it: a shift in the void currents that flowed from the sun, a change in the weight of the world.

Enlil’s successors—a new generation of Igigi who had been born in the light—measured the disturbance with instruments that had been refined over centuries. Their calculations pointed to something approaching the system. Something large. Something that was bending the gravity of the outer planets as it passed.

“A ship,” the lead Igigi, a woman named Enara, reported to the council. “Or many ships. They are decelerating. They will enter orbit within the decade.”

Mora’s granddaughter, a woman named Seren who had been named for the old singer, sat at the head of the table. She was young by the measure of the council, but her face was old with the weight of what she had inherited.

“The Annunaki,” she said.

“The same signatures,” Enara said. “The same vrill resonance that the Igigi recorded from the transport that dropped us. They are coming back.”

The council chamber was silent. Vale stood at the edge of the room, his form steady, his presence a weight that pressed against the walls. He had known this moment would come. He had been preparing for it since the day he woke.

“They are not coming for us,” he said. “Not directly. They are coming to see what grew in the garden they planted. They want to know if the darkness did its work.”

“And when they see that it did not?” Seren asked.

“Then they will decide what to do.” He looked at the Ma'at, visible through the window, pulsing with its slow, heavy rhythm. “But they are not the only ones who have been waiting.”

V — The Fifth Century: The Ma'at Speaks

The voice returned on the night the first Annunaki scout ships entered the outer system.

Vale was at the needle’s base, his hands pressed to the stone, listening to the pulse. He had been doing this for centuries now—feeling the rhythm, waiting for the change that he knew would come. The thread had been quiet for decades, the presence on the other end silent but present.

Now it spoke.

They are coming.

He opened his eyes. The Ma'at pulsed in the distance, its light brighter than it had been in centuries.

“I know.”

You are not ready.

“No.”

We are not ready.

The voice was different this time. Less like a challenge, more like an acknowledgment. Vale felt the weight of centuries in it—the same weight he carried, the same slow accumulation of becoming.

“What do you want?” he asked.

To survive. The same as you. The same as all of us.

He stood. The night was cool, the stars bright, the first of the Annunaki ships too distant to see but present in the currents that flowed through the world.

“Then we need to talk. Before they get here.”

The thread pulsed. For a moment, Vale felt the presence on the other end hesitate—not in refusal, but in the way something hesitates before a door it has been afraid to open for seven thousand years.

Yes.

The word was soft, almost lost in the pulse. But it was there. And in the silence that followed, Vale felt something that had been waiting for centuries finally begin to move.

VI — The Threshold

The morning sun rose over Garden Grove, and the city went about its work.

The fields were planted. The walls were repaired. The songs were sung. But there was a tension in the air now, a waiting that had not been there before. The Annunaki were coming. The Ma'at had spoken. And in the centuries between, something had changed.

Vale stood on the eastern wall, watching the light spread across the marsh. Beside him stood Elara, her eyes closed, her hands tracing the currents that flowed from the needle to the Ma'at.

“They are building a bridge,” she said. “The swamp giants. Through the web they have been weaving. They are making a way for us to come to them.”

“Not a weapon.”

“No.” She opened her eyes. “A door. They have been afraid for so long. But now they are more afraid of what is coming than they are of us.”

Vale looked at the Ma'at, at the dark shape that had been an enemy for seven thousand years. He thought of the voice, the hesitation, the word that had been spoken in the dark.

““We will go,” he said. “When the bridge is ready. We will go and we will talk.”

“And if they do not want to talk?”

He smiled—a strange expression on a face that had learned to have expressions over centuries. “Then we will listen. That is what we have been learning to do.”

The sun climbed higher. The city stirred behind him. And in the east, the mound pulsed with a rhythm that was no longer just opposition. It was a rhythm that was learning to change.

The Annunaki were coming. But before they arrived, something else would happen. Something that had been waiting for seven thousand years.

The door was opening.
