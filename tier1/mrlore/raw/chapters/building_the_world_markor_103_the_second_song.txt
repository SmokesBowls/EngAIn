Opening Scene: Parallel Series – The Southern Landing

The Ethereal Realm, moments after the first thousand Nephoretti leap through the Veil. The six Aeon Keepers converge in the space between spaces, their consciousnesses vibrating with residual effort.

Lyaris turned her awareness toward the southern hemisphere. The first thousand had landed cleanly—their descent guided by the steady pulse of the northern void needle, which had survived the shattering intact. But the south… she extended her perception through the vrill currents and felt it immediately: a wound in the dimensional fabric. The southern void needle, ancient and immense, had cracked during Tiamat’s destruction. Its pulse was erratic, sending shockwaves through the Veil that made the friction between realms jagged and unpredictable.

“The southern needle is compromised,” she said. “The turbulence will be… severe.”

Korath’s consciousness hardened. “Then we craft the second thousand for survival. Denser thought‑seeds. Redundant consciousness matrices. They must endure forces that would tear the first group apart.”

Theron pulsed agreement. “Probability streams show high fragmentation. Some seeds may not survive the transition. Others will scatter across hundreds of miles. The Veil in that region behaves like a storm—localized collapses, sudden density spikes.”

“And the Annunaki?” Vaelith asked. “If the needle is damaged, they cannot maintain a stable presence there. The southern continent may be empty.”

Mordain’s awareness brushed against ancient archives. “Not entirely. The void needle itself remains—a relic from before our time. The Annunaki may have attempted to study it once, but without a functional needle, they cannot hold a foothold. The southern continent has been untouched for millennia.”

A silence passed between them—the equivalent of a shared breath.

“Then we send the second thousand,” Lyaris said. “We give them resilience. And we give them something more: the instinct to seek shelter. When they land in chaos, they will need to find stability. The damaged needle, even broken, radiates a rhythm they can anchor to.”

---

The crafting took longer this time. Each thought‑seed was woven with thicker vrill strands, their consciousness‑patterns compressed into matrices that could withstand the chaotic pressures of the southern Veil. Lyaris worked with painstaking precision, layering in not only the standard Nephoretti template—geomancy, vrill‑sense, language capacity—but a quiet, persistent pull toward the damaged needle’s pulse. A sense that order could be found even in ruin.

When the thousand seeds were ready, they released them.

The descent was nothing like the first. The northern seeds had glided through the Veil like stones through calm water. These were flung into a maelstrom. The damaged needle’s erratic pulses created vortices in the dimensional fabric, twisting the vrill currents into knots. Some thought‑seeds shredded before they could form bodies. Others tore apart mid‑transition, their consciousness fragments scattering into the quantum foam.

But six hundred made it through.

Their manifesting bodies, forged denser and more resilient, crashed onto the southern continent in bursts of raw energy that lit the bruised sky like falling stars.

On the ground, Teryn opened her eyes to darkness and the smell of sulfur.

She lay in a crater of shattered rock, her newly‑formed body aching with the violence of arrival. Around her, the air shimmered with residual vrill—wild, unsteady, nothing like the stable currents the Aeon Keepers had described. She pushed herself up on trembling arms and looked out.

The landscape was jagged, volcanic. Rivers of cooling basalt snaked between black cliffs. In the distance, a mountain vented steam into a bruised sky. And there, half‑buried in a collapsed hillside, she saw it: a spire of metal and crystal, towering higher than any structure from the memory‑seeds the Aeon Keepers had implanted.

The void needle. Ancient. Broken.

Its crystalline core was exposed to the elements, fractured along a diagonal fault that leaked faint pulses of light—each one sending a shudder through the Veil above. Even damaged, it radiated a deep, rhythmic hum that Teryn could feel in her bones. A heartbeat. A wound that still pulsed.

Around her, other Nephoretti were rising from their own craters. Some moved with purpose; others stumbled, disoriented. A few craters held only scattered vrill‑ash—those who had not survived.

Teryn counted quickly. In her immediate vicinity: perhaps twenty survivors. How many more had landed beyond the ridge, she could not yet tell.

She took a step toward the broken needle, drawn by the quiet compulsion the Aeon Keepers had woven into her—not curiosity about the structure itself, but an instinct for stability. The pulse, even erratic, was the most ordered thing in this chaotic place.

As she approached, she noticed that the ground around the needle was different. Not volcanic ash, but worked stone—ancient paving, cracked and overgrown but unmistakably shaped. A plaza of some kind, built around the spire’s base. Whoever had erected this structure had intended it to be approached with reverence.

She reached the edge of the plaza and stopped. Up close, the needle’s scale was overwhelming. The fracture ran diagonally through its core, exposing layers of crystal and metal that had grown like sedimentary rock over eons. The pulse that emanated from it was slow, labored—a heart struggling to beat.

Behind her, other Nephoretti were gathering at the plaza’s edge. None spoke. The memory‑seeds they carried included language, but this moment required no words. They stood together, survivors of a violent birth, staring up at a monument from a time before memory.

Teryn felt the pulse of the broken needle resonate with something deep in her newly‑formed body. Not a call. Not a command. Simply… presence. A reminder that even in ruin, order could persist.

She turned to face the others. Twenty‑three survivors, by her count. Their faces were smudged with ash, their bodies still adjusting to the strange weight of physical form. But their eyes held the same recognition: they were not alone, and this place—broken as it was—would be their beginning.

“We build,” she said, the word coming out rough, her voice still learning itself. “We build, and we learn. The pulse of this place—it can guide us.”

No one argued. They were too exhausted, too raw, too new. But as one, they turned back toward the broken needle and began to move—not into it, but around it, spreading out to explore the ancient plaza, to find shelter among the fallen stones, to begin the work of survival.

High above, the Veil continued to churn, stirred by the needle’s erratic pulse. But here on the ground, for the first time since their violent arrival, there was stillness.

The southern continent had received them. Now it would test them

Part 2: Broken Ground

The first dawn on the southern continent came not as light but as a slow bleeding of darkness into the color of bruised fruit. Teryn watched it from the edge of the ancient plaza, her back against a fallen block of worked stone, her hands still raw from the work of survival.

Three days since the leap. Three days since she had clawed her way out of a crater and found twenty-three others breathing the sulfur air. Three days of learning what it meant to have a body that could ache, could hunger, could shiver when the wind swept down from the volcanic highlands.

The others were scattered across the plaza now, each finding their rhythm. Some gathered the glassy stones that littered the edges of the ancient paving, stacking them into low windbreaks. Others explored the perimeter where the worked stone gave way to twisted swamp—knee-deep water tinted the color of rust, trees with roots that walked, air so thick with moisture it felt like breathing through wet cloth. A few had ventured toward the broken needle itself, standing in its shadow, heads tilted as if listening to the erratic pulse that vibrated through the ground.

Kael was one of them. He stood closest to the fracture, his newly-formed features creased in concentration. Of all the survivors, he seemed most attuned to the needle’s rhythm—or perhaps most troubled by it. When Teryn approached, he didn’t turn.

“It’s slowing,” he said. His voice still had the rough edge of newness, words shaped by a throat that had never spoken before three days ago. “The pulse. It’s not steady. It… falters.”

Teryn felt it too, now that he mentioned it. A hesitation in the deep thrum that had guided them since landing. Like a heart skipping beats.

“The Aeon Keepers said it was damaged,” she said. “We knew it wouldn’t be stable.”

Kael’s jaw tightened. “Damaged implies it was whole once. This feels like dying.”

They stood in silence, watching the fractured core leak pale light into the morning. Then a shout came from the eastern edge of the plaza—one of the sentries, her voice sharp with alarm.

Teryn moved before she thought, her body responding faster than her mind. The memory-seeds the Keepers had implanted gave her knowledge of combat, of movement, but the reality of running across uneven stone with muscles that still felt foreign was something else entirely. She reached the eastern edge breathless, just in time to see what had alarmed the sentry.

Movement in the swamp. Not the slow sway of vegetation, but something deliberate. Something large.

The mist parted, and Teryn saw them.

Three figures, each taller than the fallen stones that dotted the plaza. Thirty feet at least, their bodies composed of living stone and crystallized minerals, just as the Aeon Keepers’ memories had described. Pelagor transformed by millennia of survival in Earth’s crucible. Giants.

But these were not the docile creatures the Keepers had observed on the northern continent. These moved with purpose. With aggression. Their massive hands were clenched into fists, their stony faces set in expressions that Teryn’s newly-formed instincts read as hostility.

One of them slammed its fist against a swamp tree, shattering the trunk to splinters. The sound cracked through the morning like thunder.

“Back,” Teryn said, her voice low but carrying. “Back to the needle.”

The other Nephoretti were already moving, abandoning their half-built shelters, gathering toward the center of the plaza where the broken spire loomed. But the giants were faster than their size suggested. The lead one—its hide mottled green-black with swamp growth, its limbs thicker and more jagged than the northern variants—lumbered onto the ancient paving in three strides.

It stopped at the edge of the plaza, its massive head swiveling as it took in the Nephoretti. Its eyes, deep-set and glowing faintly with internal heat, fixed on Teryn.

She stood her ground. Not bravery—she simply had nowhere to go that would matter. The others were behind her, clustered at the needle’s base. If this creature wanted to reach them, it would have to go through her.

“We mean no harm,” she said, though she had no idea if the giant could understand her. The Keepers had said the transformed Pelagor had no language, only instinct and genetic memory. But maybe tone carried meaning. Maybe intent could be felt.

The giant tilted its head, and for a moment Teryn thought she saw something in its eyes—not understanding, but recognition. Then it raised its fist.

She dove sideways as the blow came down, the stone beneath her shattering into shards that sliced her arm. She rolled, came up bleeding, and saw the other two giants advancing from the flanks. They moved with the same deliberate menace, their eyes fixed not on her but on the group clustered at the needle.

They were hunting.

“Inside!” Kael’s voice cut through the chaos. He had found a gap in the needle’s base—a fissure where the ancient structure met the ground, wide enough for a Nephoretti to squeeze through. The others were already pushing toward it, helping each other through the narrow opening.

Teryn ran, her injured arm clutched to her chest. The lead giant swung again, its fist catching the edge of a fallen stone and sending it tumbling toward her. She leaped, felt the stone clip her heel, and crashed onto the paving hard enough to knock the breath from her lungs.

A hand grabbed her arm—one of the others, a Nephoretti she hadn’t spoken to yet, wiry and quick. He hauled her upright and shoved her toward the fissure. She scrambled through the gap, feeling the rough crystal scrape her shoulders, and tumbled into darkness.

Inside the needle was not empty.

The space opened into a chamber that pulsed with the same erratic rhythm as the spire itself. Veins of crystal lined the walls, some dark and dead, others flickering with pale light. The floor was smooth, worked by hands older than memory. And the air—the air hummed.

Teryn counted heads in the dimness. Twenty-two. Two missing. She didn’t know if they had been caught outside or had scattered elsewhere. She didn’t have time to mourn.

Outside, the giants were circling the needle. Their footsteps shook the ground. One of them tried to reach into the fissure, its massive fingers scraping against the opening, but the gap was too narrow. It pulled back with a sound like grinding stones—frustration.

“They can’t get in,” someone whispered. “We’re safe.”

Kael shook his head. He was pressed against one of the crystal veins, his hand flat against its surface. “This place is dying,” he said quietly. “The pulse is weakening. When it stops…” He didn’t finish.

Teryn looked around the chamber. Twenty-two survivors, huddled in the gut of an ancient machine that was slowly failing. Outside, creatures that should have been kin were trying to kill them.

“The Keepers said the northern giants were docile,” she said. “What happened here?”

No one had an answer. But as she sat in the fading light of the damaged needle, listening to the giants’ heavy tread circling above, Teryn understood one thing clearly: the southern continent was not a place of peaceful rebuilding. It was a trial. And they were not prepared.

The needle’s pulse faltered again, a longer hesitation this time. In the silence that followed, Teryn heard something new—a sound from deeper within the structure. A drip, slow and rhythmic, like water finding its way through stone.

Or like something waking.

She turned toward the darkness that led deeper into the needle’s heart, and felt the first stirring of a new kind of fear.

Part 3: The Gathering Pulse

The sound that had shifted the needle’s pulse came from Sera.

She hadn’t meant to make it. Huddled against the crystal wall, her hands pressed over her ears to block out the giants’ footsteps, she had begun humming—a low, wavering note that vibrated in her throat without conscious intent. It was the sound her mother had used on Tiamat, three thousand years gone, to quiet her when the tidal storms shook their dwelling. A sound she shouldn’t remember, because she had never had a mother, had never been a child, had been born three days ago from thought and vrill and the will of the Aeon Keepers.

But the memory was there, woven into her like all the others. And the sound came out.

The pulse changed.

It was subtle—a hesitation, then a shift in rhythm, the erratic beat smoothing into something almost steady. The crystal veins along the walls flickered, their pale light strengthening for a breath, then fading again. But the shift was enough. The giants’ footsteps above paused. A long silence. Then, slowly, the heavy tread receded.

Sera stopped humming. The pulse returned to its irregular, labored beat.

Kael’s hand was pressed flat against the crystal vein beside him. His eyes were wide. “It responded,” he said, his voice barely a whisper. “To you.”

Sera stared at him, then at her own hands. “I didn’t… I don’t know what I did.”

No one had an explanation. But when Teryn led them back out into the fading light, the plaza was empty. The giants had withdrawn into the swamp, their tracks leading east, back toward the volcanic highlands. Two Nephoretti lay motionless near the plaza’s edge—those who hadn’t made it inside. The others stood in silence, looking at the broken bodies, at the shattered stone, at the spire that still pulsed behind them.

Teryn knelt beside the fallen. Their faces were peaceful, their forms already beginning to dissolve into vrill-ash. She didn’t know their names. She hadn’t had time to learn them.

“We need to be ready when they come back,” Kael said. “They will come back.”

They worked through the night. Fallen stones from the plaza’s edge were dragged into rough walls, stacked between the broken paving and the needle’s base. Those with stronger geomantic sense—and all of them had some, the Keepers had made sure of that—learned to shape stone with their hands, pressing vrill into the cracks until the blocks fused. It was crude work, nothing like the mountain cities of Tiamat that lived in their memory-seeds, but it held.

By dawn, they had a wall. Low, barely chest-high, but enough to slow an approach. Enough to give them time to reach the fissure.

More survivors arrived that day.

They came from the swamps, from the volcanic slopes, from the rust-colored rivers that cut through the lowlands. Some had landed close, hidden in the roots of the walking trees, waiting for the giants to leave. Others had walked for days, following the needle’s pulse like a beacon. Each arrival brought new skills, new knowledge, new questions.

“There are hundreds of us,” said a Nephoretti named Jaren, who had counted thirty-seven in his group before they found the plaza. “Scattered across the lowlands. Some didn’t survive the landing. Others…” He looked toward the highlands. “Others landed further south, near the fire mountains. I don’t know if they made it.”

Teryn marked the directions on the plaza’s stone floor with a charred stick. “How many did you see fall?”

“Enough.” Jaren’s voice was flat. “The turbulence was worse the further south we landed. I saw thought-seeds tear apart before they could form bodies. And the giants…” He shook his head. “They’re everywhere. Not like the Keepers described. They hunt.”

By the end of the second day, nearly a hundred Nephoretti had gathered at the plaza. The low wall had grown into a proper barrier, and those with stronger geomantic sense had begun to shape stone into shelters against the needle’s base. The pulse continued its uneven rhythm, but when Sera hummed again—testing, uncertain—it steadied for a moment. The others noticed. Some began to hum along. The pulse responded, stronger this time, and the crystal veins along the needle’s exterior glowed faintly in the twilight.

“It likes sound,” Kael said, watching the light fade. “Or it needs it. I don’t know which.”

They didn’t have time to explore the mystery. On the third morning, the giants returned.

Part 4: The Fire Mountain Group

Five hundred miles south, where the continent fractured into a labyrinth of volcanic vents and obsidian cliffs, a different gathering was taking place.

Renn had landed badly.

His thought-seed had torn through the Veil with a violence that left him unconscious for two days, his body half-formed and bleeding vrill into the scorched earth. When he finally woke, he was alone. The crater around him was filled with ash and shattered crystal, and the sky above was the color of iron.

He had followed the pulse north—or what he thought was north. The needle’s signal was weaker here, distorted by the volcanic activity, but he could feel it: a distant heartbeat, calling him toward stability. He walked for a day before he found the others.

They were six, huddled in a cave system that cut through the volcanic rock. Their leader was a Nephoretti named Deryn, who had landed with a group of twelve. Five hadn’t survived the first night.

“The giants found us before we could find shelter,” Deryn said. Her voice was raw, her arm bandaged with torn cloth. “They came out of the steam. We didn’t even see them until…”

She didn’t finish. Renn didn’t need her to. The marks were everywhere—clawed stone, shattered crystals, the faint vrill-ash that marked where a Nephoretti had dissolved back into raw potential.

“There are more of us,” Renn said. “North. Near the needle. I felt it before the volcanoes started interfering.”

Deryn looked at her remaining five. They were exhausted, injured, their geomancy too weak to shape the hostile stone around them. “We can’t make that journey. The giants patrol the lowlands. And the terrain between here and the needle…” She shook her head.

They spent the next three days in the caves, surviving on what little vrill they could draw from the volcanic vents. The giants came twice, their massive forms silhouetted against the smoke, their stony faces expressionless. The Nephoretti hid in the deepest crevices, pressing themselves into the rock, willing themselves to be silent.

On the fourth day, they heard singing.

It was faint, carried on the wind from somewhere to the north. Not Nephoretti voices—something deeper, more resonant. The giants heard it too. They turned toward the sound, their massive heads cocked, and for a long moment they stood motionless. Then, as one, they began to move east, away from the caves, away from the Nephoretti.

“What was that?” Deryn whispered.

Renn didn’t know. But when the giants had disappeared into the steam, he led them out of the caves and began the journey north. They moved at night, when the volcanic glare was muted and the giants were less active. They lost one more—a Nephoretti named Elara, who slipped on a obsidian slope and fell into a crevasse too deep to reach.

By the time they reached the lowlands, their group had grown to fourteen. Other survivors had emerged from the cracks and craters, drawn by the same instinct, following the same distant pulse. They moved together, silent and watchful, learning to read the land: where the giants hunted, where the ground was stable, where the vrill currents pooled in pockets of relative calm.

On the seventh day, they saw the needle.

It rose from the swamp like a broken bone, its upper reaches lost in the haze. Around its base, lights flickered—not the erratic pulse of a damaged system, but something steadier. Something organized.

“They’ve built,” Deryn said, and there was wonder in her voice.

As they approached the ancient plaza, they saw the wall, the shelters, the Nephoretti moving with purpose. And at the center, standing at the needle’s base, a woman with dark hair and steady eyes watched them come.

Teryn counted them as they entered: fourteen survivors, worn and wounded, their faces carrying the weight of what they had endured. Behind them, more figures emerged from the swamp—stragglers from other groups, drawn by the same beacon.

“We’re not all here,” Renn said when he reached her. His voice cracked from days of silence. “There are others. South. West. Scattered. Some didn’t make it.”

Teryn nodded. She had expected this. The Keepers had sent a thousand, and six hundred had survived the jump. Of those, perhaps half would find their way here. The rest were out there, alone, surviving or not.

“We’ll find them,” she said. “We’ll build, and we’ll find them.”

She looked up at the needle, at the fading light pulsing in its broken core. Somewhere in that ancient structure, something was listening. Something that had responded to Sera’s humming, that had steadied when voices joined together.

She didn’t know what it was. None of them did. But it was there, patient and waiting, and one day they would understand.

For now, there was work to do. The giants would return. The land would test them. And somewhere out there, other Nephoretti were making their own journeys, finding their own places to stand.

The Second Song was only beginning

Part 5: The Safe Perimeter

In the days that followed, the plaza transformed.

What had been a scattering of fallen stones became something approaching a settlement. The wall grew from chest-high to a proper rampart, fused stone reinforced with vrill-charged crystal salvaged from the needle’s outer fractures. Shelters multiplied against the spire’s base—not the crude windbreaks of the first week, but proper structures with sloping roofs and interior spaces divided by woven reeds from the swamp’s edge.

Teryn walked the perimeter each morning, her hand brushing the wall’s surface, feeling the faint vibration of the needle’s pulse through the stone. It was steadier now, not by much, but enough. And something else: the giants never came close.

“They stop at the old road,” Kael reported one evening, pointing to a line of moss-covered stones that marked the eastern boundary of the plaza’s original extent. “I’ve watched them for three days. They patrol the lowlands, they hunt along the river, but they don’t cross that line.”

Jaren had made similar observations to the north, where the swamp gave way to a ridge of volcanic rock. “There’s a clear boundary. They circle the area, but they don’t enter. It’s like the needle’s pulse pushes them back.”

“Then why did they attack us at all?” someone asked.

Teryn considered. “Maybe the pulse was weaker then. Or maybe they were testing—seeing if the needle’s protection still held.” She looked at the broken spire. “If they’ve been here for millennia, they know what this place is. They know it’s wounded.”

The implication settled over them: the needle kept them safe, but only so long as its pulse endured. And its pulse was still erratic, still weakening, still dependent on forces they didn’t understand.

Sera hummed more often now. Not intentionally—it had become a habit, a background rhythm to her work. Others had picked it up, coordinating their movements to the pulse’s beat, their voices rising in wordless song as they shaped stone or wove reeds or stood watch. The pulse responded each time, steadying, its light strengthening for a few heartbeats before fading again.

No one spoke of it as magic. It was simply something that happened when they worked together, when their voices joined. A thing they were learning, without quite knowing how.

Part 6: The Outer Structures

On the tenth day, they began to explore beyond the immediate plaza.

The ground surrounding the needle was not natural. Millennia of swamp growth had buried much of it, but beneath the muck and twisted roots, they found evidence of something larger—a complex that extended outward in concentric rings, its outer edges lost in the dense vegetation.

“This wasn’t just a tower,” Kael said, standing at the edge of what might have been a courtyard. Fallen columns lay half-submerged in black water, their surfaces carved with patterns that predated anything in the Aeon Keepers’ memory-seeds. “This was a city. Or the beginning of one.”

They cleared the nearest structures first: a ring of low buildings that might have been workshops or dwellings, their stone roofs collapsed but their walls still standing. Inside, they found nothing but sediment and the roots of swamp trees—but the walls themselves held traces of vrill, faint as old scars.

“The Keepers didn’t build this,” Jaren said, running his hand over a carving that seemed to shift under his fingers. “This is older. Much older.”

Teryn remembered the Aeon Keepers’ words: The void needles were here before us. She hadn’t understood what that meant until now. This place had been ancient when Tiamat was young. And something had lived here, something that understood vrill in ways she was only beginning to sense.

The exploration yielded little else that week—the swamp was aggressive, reclaiming the old stones faster than they could clear them—but it gave them something they hadn’t had before: a sense of scale. The needle was not alone. It was the heart of something that had once spread across this land, and whatever had built it had intended it to last.

“We should send scouts further out,” Renn suggested one evening. He had recovered from his journey, though his arm still bore the scar where the giant’s claw had nearly torn it off. “If we’re safe here, others might be looking for us. They need to know this place exists.”

Teryn agreed. “Groups of three. Stay within a day’s travel. Mark the safe paths, note where the giants are thickest. And if you find survivors—” She paused. “Bring them back.”

The scouts left at dawn, moving in different directions: east toward the river, north along the volcanic ridge, south into the deeper swamp, and west where the land rose toward forested hills. Each carried a small crystal from the needle’s outer fractures, its faint pulse a thread they could follow home.

Part 7: The Delta Group

Seventy miles west, where the continent’s spine fractured into a maze of tidal channels and mangrove forests, a different story was unfolding.

Venn had never been meant to lead.

She had been crafted with the same thought-seed as all the others—resilience, geomancy, the quiet pull toward the needle’s pulse—but something in her landing had sharpened her instincts. Or perhaps it was simply survival.

Her group had landed in the delta, a tangle of salt water and twisted roots that stretched as far as any of them could see. Thirty-seven had come through the Veil together. Eighteen had survived the first hour, drowned in the channels or crushed by falling mangrove trunks. The rest had scattered, some swept away by currents, others dragged under by things that moved in the murk.

By the time Venn had found dry ground—a hummock of ancient mud packed hard as stone—she was alone.

She had followed the needle’s pulse west. Or thought she had. But the delta distorted everything. The pulse was a ghost here, reflected and refracted by the water, coming from every direction and none. She walked for three days before she found the others.

They were twelve, huddled in the roots of a massive mangrove that had grown around what might once have been a stone foundation. Their leader, a woman named Ria, had organized them into watches and work details, but they were barely surviving. The giants here were different—smaller, faster, adapted to the channels. They moved through the water with a silence that made them invisible until they struck.

“We lost seven in the first week,” Ria told Venn when they finally had a moment to speak. “They come at night, sometimes. Or during the high tide, when the water covers our paths. We’ve learned to climb, to stay in the high branches where they can’t reach. But we can’t build here. Can’t grow.”

Venn looked at the survivors. They were thin, their clothes torn, their faces hollow. The needle’s pulse was a distant memory to them, too weak to guide, too faint to anchor.

“There’s a place,” Venn said. “East. The pulse is stronger there. The Keepers said the needle would protect us.”

“The needle is broken,” Ria said flatly. “We felt it when we landed. It’s dying.”

“It’s still there. And there are others—we can’t be the only ones who survived.”

Ria looked at her people, then at the dark water surrounding their refuge. “We can’t travel. The giants patrol the channels. And half of us can’t swim.”

“Then we teach them,” Venn said. “Or we build rafts. Or we find another way. But we can’t stay here.”

The argument continued for two more days. On the third, a giant found their refuge.

It came at dusk, moving through the channels with a predator’s patience. The sentry saw it first—a flash of movement in the water—but before she could cry out, the giant’s arm swept up and smashed the branch she was standing on. She fell screaming, and the water closed over her.

The others scattered, climbing higher into the mangrove’s tangle. The giant circled below, its stony hide scraping against the trunk, its eyes scanning the branches. It was smaller than the ones Venn had glimpsed from a distance—perhaps twenty feet—but in the tight confines of the delta, it was unstoppable.

Venn found herself pressed against a branch, her heart pounding, her breath shallow. Below, the giant’s hand closed around a root and pulled, testing the tree’s strength. The whole structure groaned.

“It’s going to bring it down,” Ria whispered from somewhere above.

Venn looked at the survivors scattered around her. Fifteen now. Fifteen against one giant, with no weapons, no walls, nowhere to run. The thought-seeds the Keepers had given them included knowledge of combat, but theory and reality were different things.

“We have to hit it,” she said, surprising herself. “Together. Use the vrill—shape the branches, drop them on its head. It’s small. It can be hurt.”

Ria stared at her. “We can’t shape stone. There’s no stone here.”

“Then we shape the wood. The water. We shape what we have.”

She didn’t wait for an answer. She reached down into the vrill current that flowed through the delta—faint, unstable, but present—and pressed her will into the branch beneath her. It was like trying to catch water with bare hands. The vrill slipped through her fingers, refusing to take shape.

Below, the giant pulled again. The mangrove shrieked.

“Now!” Venn shouted, and she threw herself into the vrill with everything she had.

The branch didn’t shape. But it moved—a fraction, a twist—and that was enough. Around her, others were doing the same, their fear sharpening their focus, their combined will finding purchase in the unstable current. Branches shifted, roots writhed, and for a moment, the vrill responded.

The giant looked up, its stony face registering something that might have been surprise. Then a branch the size of a man’s torso swung down and caught it across the head.

It didn’t fall. But it staggered. And in that moment, Venn saw something she hadn’t expected: fear. The giant—this creature that had hunted them for weeks, that had killed seven of their number—was afraid.

“Again!” she screamed.

The survivors threw their will into the vrill, and the mangrove answered. Branches lashed, roots coiled, and the giant retreated, splashing back into the channel, its massive arms raised to ward off blows. Within seconds, it was gone, swallowed by the darkness.

Part 9: The Fortress at the Needle

By the end of the second month, the settlement around the broken needle had become something more than a refuge. It was a fortress, a workshop, a seed of what might one day grow into a city.

The outer wall had grown to twice its original height, fused stone reinforced with vrill-charged crystal salvaged from the needle’s base. Watchtowers rose at each cardinal point, their platforms woven from swamp wood and bound with geomancy strong enough to hold against the heaviest storms. Within the walls, shelters had become proper dwellings—roofed with thatch, divided into rooms, their walls carved with patterns that each Nephoretti had brought from the memory‑seeds of Tiamat.

Two hundred and thirty‑seven survivors now called the plaza home. They had come from every direction: the delta rafts, the eastern hills, the northern ridge where volcanic vents made the ground tremble. Each arrival brought new skills, new stories, new understanding of the land they were learning to inhabit.

Teryn walked the perimeter at dawn, as she had every morning since the first wall went up. The pulse of the needle vibrated through the stone beneath her feet—still erratic, still faltering, but steadier than it had been. The rhythm seemed to match the settlement’s own heartbeat: the sounds of voices raised in work‑song, the clatter of stone‑shaping, the low hum that always seemed to underlie their days now.

Sera had become something of a keeper of that hum. She sang while she worked, wordless melodies that rose and fell with the pulse. Others had begun to join her—not consciously, but because the sound felt right. When enough voices blended, the needle’s light brightened. The pulse quickened. Sometimes, for a few heartbeats, the broken spire would hum in answer.

No one understood it. But no one questioned it, either. It was simply part of life now, like the morning mist that rose from the swamp or the way the giants never crossed the ancient stone boundary that marked the plaza’s edge.

“The scouts from the southern ridge returned,” Kael said, falling into step beside her. He had become her de facto second, his sensitivity to the needle’s rhythms making him indispensable. “They found signs of survivors. A group of perhaps thirty, sheltering in the caves below the fire mountains.”

“They’ve been there since the landing. Too afraid to move, too weak to travel far.” Kael’s expression was troubled. “But the report isn’t just about survivors. The giants in the highlands are different, Teryn. Smaller, maybe twenty feet, but faster. More organized. They don’t hunt alone.”

She resumed walking, her mind already working through implications. “Different how?”

“The scouts saw them using tools. Stone axes. Battering rams. And they work together—flanking maneuvers, coordinated attacks. They’ve been herding the survivors, keeping them pinned in the caves.”

Teryn felt a cold weight settle in her chest. The giants they had fought at the plaza had been powerful but simple—brute force, not tactics. If there were others who hunted with strategy, the survivors in the highlands wouldn’t last much longer.

“We need to bring them in,” she said. “Can we send a relief party?”

Kael shook his head slowly. “Not without leaving the settlement vulnerable. The perimeter patrols have reported increased activity. The big ones are testing the boundary again—not attacking, but circling. Watching. They know we’re here, and they’re waiting.”

“Then we split the difference.” Teryn turned to face him. “We send a small group—fast, skilled, capable of navigating the highlands. They find the survivors, guide them back. Meanwhile, we hold here and let the big ones know this place isn’t worth the cost of taking.”

Kael considered. “I know who should lead the relief. Renn. He came through the highlands. He knows the terrain.”

“And he lost people there. He’ll want to go back.” Teryn nodded. “Make it happen. Six volunteers, no more. They move at night, travel light, don’t engage unless they have to. Bring those survivors home.”

Part 10: The Grove Giants

Renn’s group left at dusk, six Nephoretti moving like shadows through the western marsh. They carried nothing but crystal shards from the needle—pulse‑anchors that would guide them back—and the knowledge that every day they spent in the highlands was a day the settlement stood with fewer defenders.

The journey took three days. The terrain shifted from swamp to rocky foothills, then to a landscape of black basalt and sulfurous steam vents that hissed like angry serpents. Here, the needle’s pulse was a ghost, barely perceptible, and the vrill currents ran wild and unpredictable.

They found the survivors on the fourth morning—thirty‑two Nephoretti huddled in a cave system that wound deep into the volcanic rock. Their leader was a woman named Nessa, whose sharp features and steady eyes spoke of someone who had learned to make hard decisions.

“We thought we were the only ones,” she said when Renn explained their mission. “We saw the lights in the sky when you landed, but the giants—” She gestured toward the cave entrance, where the distant shape of a grove giant could be seen patrolling the valley below. “They didn’t let us leave.”

“How many?” Renn asked.

“Started with sixty. Lost eight in the first week to the giants. The rest…” Nessa’s jaw tightened. “The rest we lost to the caves. To the heat. To not knowing which way was safe.”

Thirty-two survivors from sixty. Renn did the math in his head and felt the weight of it. They had lost nearly half, and the ones who remained were thin, their bioluminescence dim, their vrill‑sense dulled by constant fear.

“We’ll get you out,” he said. “But we need to know what we’re dealing with. The giants here—the scouts said they were different.”

Nessa led him to a crevice that overlooked the valley. Below, a grove of stone trees rose from the volcanic soil—not living trees, but formations that mimicked the shape of ancient oaks, their trunks fused with crystal. And moving among them, a giant.

It was smaller than the ones that had attacked the plaza—perhaps twenty feet—but its body was denser, more compact. Its hide was the color of cooled lava, shot through with veins of obsidian that caught the morning light. In its hands, it carried a stone axe, the blade knapped from volcanic glass with a precision that spoke of intelligence, not instinct.

“There are three of them,” Nessa whispered. “They take turns patrolling. When one leaves, another comes. They’ve been doing it for weeks.”

“And they haven’t attacked?”

“They don’t need to. They’re waiting for us to come out. Every time we tried to leave, they drove us back. Not killing—herding. Like they’re…” She struggled for the word. “Corralling us.”

Renn watched the grove giant move through the stone forest, its movements deliberate, patient. This was not the mindless aggression of the swamp giants. This was hunting with purpose.

“We’re not leaving the way we came,” he said. “We go through the caves. There has to be another exit.”

It took them two more days to find a route through the volcanic tunnels, following the faintest pulses of vrill that seeped through cracks in the rock. They emerged on the far side of the grove, in a ravine that led down toward the lowlands. Thirty-two survivors, all exhausted, all terrified, but alive.

They traveled by night, avoiding the patrols, moving in absolute silence. On the third night, they felt it: the needle’s pulse, faint but unmistakable, calling them home.

Behind them, the grove giants did not follow. But Renn had seen the way they watched, the way they communicated with gestures and sounds that were almost language. They were not mindless. They were waiting, and when the Nephoretti returned to the highlands—as they eventually would—the grove giants would be ready.

Part 11: The Cliffs

While Renn’s group made their slow way back to the needle, another drama was unfolding far to the east, where the continent dropped away into sheer cliffs overlooking a churning gray sea.

Mira had landed on a ledge forty feet above the water, her thought‑seed slamming into the rock with enough force to fracture it. She had lain there for two days, her body slowly solidifying, before she could move.

She was alone.

The pulse from the needle was faint here, distorted by the iron-rich cliffs and the constant crash of waves. But it was there—a thread she could follow, if she could just reach stable ground. The problem was the cliffs themselves. She had climbed rock faces in the memory‑seeds the Keepers had given her, but those were memories, not experience. Her hands were bleeding before she’d climbed twenty feet.

On the third day, she heard something above her.

Voices. Not Nephoretti—something deeper, more resonant. She pressed herself against the rock, heart pounding, and listened. The sounds were rhythmic, almost like chanting, and they seemed to come from a ledge somewhere above.

She climbed toward them.

What she found was a cave opening, wide enough to admit a giant, its entrance worn smooth by ages of use. Inside, a fire burned—actual fire, fed by driftwood and something that gave off a sweet, smoky scent. And around the fire, four figures.

They were Nephoretti. Three women and a man, their bioluminescence dim but steady, their bodies whole.

“We saw you fall,” said the eldest, a woman with silver‑grey skin and eyes that had seen too much. “We’ve been watching ever since. Hoping you’d find us.”

Her name was Ione, and she had landed with a group of forty. Twenty‑three had survived the first week. The rest had been taken by the cliffs, by the cold, by the creatures that hunted the tidal pools. Now they were six—seven, counting Mira.

“We can’t stay here,” Ione said. “The winter storms are coming. We’ve seen them before. The waves will reach this cave within a month. We need to go inland, find the others.”

Mira looked at the survivors. They were tough—the cliffs had made them so—but they were also few. Six against the journey to the needle, through terrain they didn’t know, with giants that might be anywhere.

“Then we go now,” she said. “We follow the coast until we find a way up. Then we find the pulse and we follow it home.”

It took them twelve days. They climbed cliffs, crossed beaches patrolled by giants that moved like shadows, hid in caves when the storms came. They lost two more—one to a fall, one to a giant that emerged from the mist before they could react. But the others pressed on, driven by the pulse that grew stronger with each mile.

When they finally crested the last ridge and saw the needle rising from the swamp, its broken spire catching the sunset, Mira felt something she hadn’t allowed herself to feel since landing: hope.

Below, in the plaza, lights flickered. Voices rose in the evening work‑song, and the needle’s pulse seemed to beat in answer.

Part 12 Convergence

Part 13 Winter's Breath

The first sign came from the mountains.

He stood at the edge of the plaza, one hand pressed against the fused stone of the wall, and watched the peaks to the south where the first snow was beginning to dust the volcanic rock.

“It’s coming,” he said when Teryn found him. “Winter. The cold will reach us within a week.”

Part 15: Rising Water

They moved through the twisting passages as fast as the weak survivors could manage, following the faint pulse of the crystal shards that guided them toward the exit. But the water was faster.

It rose around their ankles first, then their knees, then their waists. The passages that had been dry an hour ago were now narrow channels of icy seawater, and the roar of the storm outside had become a constant thunder that vibrated through the rock.

“The exit is blocked!” one of the survivors shouted, her voice cracking with panic. “Water’s coming in from both ends!”

Renn forced himself to think. The crystal shards still glowed, but the pulse was distorted, echoing off the flooded passages. He closed his eyes, focused, and felt it: a different current, a deeper vibration that wasn’t coming from the needle at all.

“There,” he said, pointing to a narrow opening that angled upward. “That passage. It’s above the waterline.”

“We don’t know where it goes,” Sera said.

“We don’t have a choice.”

They climbed, the passage narrowing until they had to crawl, the roar of the storm fading behind them. The rock walls were slick with condensation, and in the darkness, Sera’s humming became the only light, her voice echoing strangely off the stone.

Then the passage opened.

They emerged into a chamber that took their breath away.

It was vast, a natural cathedral carved by water over eons. Stalactites hung from the ceiling like frozen lightning, and at the center of the chamber was a pool of perfectly still water, its surface reflecting light that seemed to come from nowhere. Crystal formations lined the walls, and in their depths, something moved—slow pulses of light, rhythmic and deliberate, like a heartbeat.

Sera’s humming stopped. The crystals responded, their pulses quickening, and for a moment the entire chamber vibrated with a sound that was not quite music, not quite language.

“What is this place?” Theran whispered.

Renn had no answer. He felt the pulse in his own chest, resonating with something vast and patient, something that had been here long before the Nephoretti, before the Aeon Keepers, before anything that could be called memory.

But there was no time to explore. Behind them, water was beginning to seep into the chamber, trickling down the passage they had climbed.

“We need to keep moving,” he said, forcing his voice steady. “There has to be another exit.”

Sera began to hum again, and this time the crystals answered. Their light intensified, forming patterns that seemed to point toward a narrow fissure in the far wall. The survivors moved toward it, climbing over fallen stone and ancient flowstone formations.

The water rose faster now, pouring into the chamber with a sound like a waterfall. Renn helped the last of the survivors through the fissure, then turned to see Sera standing at the edge of the pool, her hand outstretched toward the still water.

“Sera!”

She didn’t seem to hear him. Her humming had become a melody he had never heard before, something ancient and sorrowful, and the pool was responding. Ripples spread across its surface, and beneath them, something stirred—a shape, vast and indistinct, rising from the depths.

Then the water reached her knees, and she gasped, the spell broken. Renn grabbed her arm and pulled her toward the fissure as the chamber behind them filled with a sound that was almost a voice—a single, resonant tone that seemed to shake the mountain itself.

They scrambled through the fissure, scraping stone, and found themselves in a narrow chimney that led upward. Above, faint light showed—the surface, perhaps, or the last of the dying day.

They climbed.

Part 16 The Storm

They emerged on a ridge overlooking the settlement just as the first snow began to fall.

Below, the needle pulsed weakly, its light barely visible through the white. The wall was crowded with figures, and even from this distance, Renn could see the fear in their movements.

“We need to get down,” he said, but the survivors were exhausted, their strength spent.

Sera began to sing.

It wasn’t the humming she used in the settlement, or the melody she had found in the chamber. It was something new—a call, pure and simple, that rose above the wind and cut through the storm.

Below, the needle answered.

Its light flared, steadied, and for a moment the pulse was strong enough to feel even here. The figures on the wall turned toward the ridge, and Teryn’s voice echoed across the valley.

“They’re coming! Open the gate!”

They descended as the storm closed in, the snow thickening until the world was nothing but white and the faint glow of the needle ahead. Renn carried one of the survivors on his back; others leaned on each other, stumbling, falling, rising again.

By the time they reached the gate, the storm was full upon them. Teryn was there, pulling them through, counting heads, her face tight with worry.

“Fifteen,” Renn said. “All of them.”

“And you.” She looked past him, at Sera, whose face was pale and streaked with tears. “What happened out there?”

Sera shook her head. “I don’t know. There was a chamber. A pool. Something… something was there.”

“Later,” Renn said, his voice harsh. “The storm is here. We need to get inside.”

They sheltered in the needle’s base, where the walls were thickest and the pulse could be felt through the stone. Two hundred and eighty-seven survivors now, huddled together against the cold, their voices rising in a work‑song that had become something more—a hymn, perhaps, or a prayer.

Teryn stood at the edge of the chamber, watching the pulse flicker in the crystal veins. Kael was beside her, his hand pressed against the wall.

“It’s holding,” he said. “Barely. But the cold is slowing it. If the storm lasts more than a few days…”

“It will last as long as it lasts.” Teryn’s voice was steady, but her eyes were fixed on the darkness beyond the walls. “We’ll survive. We’ve survived worse.”

“Have we?” Kael asked. “We’ve only been here three months.”

Behind them, Sera sat with Renn, her voice a thread of melody that wove through the pulse. She was singing the song she had found in the chamber, and the crystal veins were responding, their light brightening, their rhythm steadying.

“For us. For the right song.” Sera looked at her hands, then at the pulse flickering in the walls. “The pool is rising. When the water recedes, we can go back. We need to go back.”

Teryn nodded slowly. She didn’t understand what Sera had found, but she understood what it meant: the southern continent held secrets that could change everything. And those secrets were waiting, patient as stone, for the moment when the song was strong enough to wake them.

Outside, the storm howled. Inside, the survivors sang.

And the needle’s pulse beat on, steady for now, waiting for the tide to turn
